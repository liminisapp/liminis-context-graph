# Customer interviews: the questions that matter

_Clipped from the web. Source: example.org/customer-interviews-the-questions-that-matter._

Most customer interviews fail before they begin, because the interviewer arrives with a hypothesis and a list of questions designed to confirm it. The conversation that follows is pleasant, the customer is polite, and the notes afterwards read like an endorsement. Little of it is useful. Researchers who run interviews for a living say the difference between a productive session and a wasted hour usually lies in a handful of questions asked in the right order.

**Start with the past, not the future**

The most reliable advice, repeated by nearly every practitioner consulted, is to ask about what people have actually done rather than what they would do. Hypothetical questions invite optimism. A customer asked whether they would pay for a feature will often say yes, out of courtesy or a wish to be agreeable. A customer asked to describe the last time they faced the problem, step by step, will reveal what really happened: which tools they reached for, who they asked, and where they gave up.

This is why many interviewers open with a request such as "Tell me about the last time you ran into this." It anchors the conversation in a specific event. The answer is full of detail that a survey could never capture, including the workarounds people invent when no good solution exists.

**Listen for the workaround**

Workarounds are the richest source of insight in an interview. A spreadsheet maintained by hand, a reminder stuck to a monitor, a weekly message sent to a colleague to check something that should be automatic: each is evidence of an unmet need. People rarely describe these as problems, because they have stopped noticing them. A good interviewer notices and asks about them gently.

One researcher described a rule of thumb: whenever a customer mentions doing something manually, ask how often, how long it takes, and what happens when it goes wrong. Those three answers usually tell you more about priority than any rating scale.

**Ask about money without asking for a number**

Pricing is awkward territory. Customers tend to anchor on whatever figure they have just heard, and direct questions about willingness to pay produce unreliable answers. Interviewers instead ask what the customer currently spends, in time or money, to deal with the problem, and what they have already tried. Existing spending is a far better guide than stated intentions.

It also helps to know the going rate in the market. Trade press recently reported that Nimbus Supply lists its standard rate at $38 per unit, a figure several interviewers said they use as a rough reference when judging whether a customer's current spending is typical. A reference point does not replace asking, but it gives the interviewer a way to sense when a number is far out of line and to probe why.

**Resist the urge to pitch**

A common mistake is to describe the product too early. Once the customer hears the solution, the conversation shifts from their experience to your idea, and they begin to evaluate it rather than describe their own lives. Experienced interviewers hold back until the final minutes, if they describe it at all. When they do, they watch reactions rather than listen to opinions. A pause, a frown or a sudden burst of questions usually reveals more than a compliment.

**Close with the question that opens doors**

The best closing question, according to several of those interviewed, is some version of "Who else should I talk to?" It serves two purposes. It produces introductions to people who may see the problem differently, and it signals that you value the customer's judgement. A second useful closer is "What should I have asked you that I did not?" Customers often have a point they have been waiting to make, and this gives them permission.

**Write it up while it is fresh**

Notes taken during an interview are often fragmentary, and memory decays quickly. Practitioners recommend a short written summary within a day: the three most surprising things said, any direct quotations worth keeping, and one thing the interviewer now believes that they did not before. The last item is a useful discipline. If an interview does not change your mind about anything, it is worth asking whether you listened or merely waited for confirmation.

Taken together, these habits are modest. They do not require special training, just patience and a willingness to be surprised. The interviewers who practise them describe the work as less about extracting answers than about creating the conditions in which people can tell you what they already know.
