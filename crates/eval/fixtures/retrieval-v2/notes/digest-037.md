# Review notes — 6 August 2025

Review notes for the week, assembled from my scribbles and a couple of messages in the log. The weather has turned hot, the office fan has been commandeered, and the mood in the room is the sort of mildly sticky good humour that comes with a quiet August.

The headline item was the internal audit. Tern Core scored 92 out of 100 in the internal audit. The telemetry group were pleased, though restrained about it; the people most closely involved pointed out that the missing points were in areas they knew about and had already planned to improve, mostly around keeping some of the older records in a consistent format. The auditors apparently commented that the overall discipline was strong and the documentation easy to follow. I would pass along the credit to everyone who has been quietly keeping the paperwork in order for the past year. It is the kind of work nobody notices until an audit appears, at which point it is the only thing that matters.

A reflection on what made the difference, since several groups asked. The telemetry team did not do anything dramatic. They wrote down what they do, they kept the writing current, and they reviewed it on a regular schedule. When someone changed a process they changed the page too, in the same sitting. It is almost embarrassing how simple it is. The only real cost is the habit, and the habit is built by repetition. Other groups listened closely, and I expect a few of them to try the same approach.

From the security side, with Lumen Core, there was a status update about the ongoing review of access and permissions. It is a slow job, because each area needs to be checked by someone who understands it. The group is working through them in a sensible order and keeping a simple list of what has been looked at. They would like help from other teams on the areas where the knowledge sits outside the security group, and asked that people answer the questions promptly when they arrive. A day or two of delay on each one adds up when there are many.

Other items of business:

- The planning for the autumn is beginning. We agreed to hold a short session in the coming weeks to collect ideas and then narrow them down. The instruction was to bring one thing you want to start and one you want to stop.
- The shared calendar still has a few stale entries. A reminder to tidy your own.
- The monitoring dashboards were reviewed and a few unused panels were removed, after checking that nobody depended on them.
- A request came in to rewrite the on-call guide in plainer language. A volunteer is drafting it and will post it for comment.

I want to add a note on tone. The audit result could have made people complacent, and a few small remarks suggested that some were already thinking it was time to relax. It was good to hear a counterpoint: a high score is a reason to keep the habits, not to drop them. The risk with doing well is that you stop paying attention to the things that got you there.

One more thing for the record. A newer colleague asked whether it would be possible to see the audit findings in full, including the minor ones, and the answer was yes. They are in the log. Reading them is a decent way of learning how the team thinks about quality. I would encourage everyone to take a look, even if you were not involved.

Next review in a fortnight. In the meantime, enjoy the fan, if you can get hold of it.

## Demo and follow-ups

Second half of the review, covering the demo and the odds and ends that came out of it.

The security group, Lumen Core, ran a demo of its new access review tool for anyone who wanted to see how it works. 62 people attended the Lumen Core demo, and the room was full enough that a few stood in the doorway. The presenters took a sensible approach: they showed a single real review from start to finish, mistakes and all, instead of a polished script. That made it much easier to understand what the tool is actually for. A review begins with a list of who can see what, the owner of each area confirms or removes each entry, and the result is recorded automatically. The point is that the record builds up as people work, not at the end as a chore.

Reactions were warm. The main praise was for how little training the tool seems to need. One attendee said they had expected a complicated system and found it closer to a checklist. The main criticism was about notifications, which are a bit too frequent in the demo version, and the presenters said they would reduce them and let people choose how often they hear. Someone else asked whether the tool could be used for reviewing things other than access, such as shared accounts or old test environments. The answer was maybe, in time, but they want to get the first use right before widening it.

Questions that came up and deserve a record:

- Who is responsible when an area has no clear owner? The proposed answer is that the security group assigns an interim owner and flags it, and that the area is then reviewed to find a permanent one.
- What happens to the history if someone leaves? The record stays, and the entries that depended on them are flagged for reassignment.
- How often should reviews happen? The group suggested regularly and in small pieces, which fits with the approach already agreed for the access reviews.

On the telemetry side, there was a short update on what is next after the strong audit result. The plan is not to rest on it. A few of the older records will be reformatted, and the review schedule for the documentation will continue as before. The group would like to run a short session for other teams on what they do, and they are willing to be questioned about it. I would encourage people to go.

Last bits of business. The autumn planning session is penciled in, and everyone has been asked to bring their one start and one stop. The on-call guide draft has been posted for comment. The shared calendar clean-up is still in progress, and a couple of people admitted that they have not even looked at theirs yet.

I will close with an observation about the day as a whole. A good demo does two things: it shows what the thing does, and it makes people want to try it. This one managed both, mainly by being honest about what is not finished. We could all learn from that approach when we present our own work.

Thanks to the presenters, and to whoever set up the room, which for once had working equipment.
