# Customer interviews: the questions that matter

Most people who run customer interviews come away with a pile of pleasant remarks and very little they can act on. The cause is rarely the customer. It is the questions, which tend to invite compliments rather than facts.

The first rule is to ask about the past, not the future. Saying "would you use this?" yields a polite yes. Asking "when did you last deal with this problem, and what did you do?" yields a story, and stories contain the details that matter: what triggered the problem, what workarounds were tried, who else was involved, and what it cost in time or money.

The second is to follow the thread. A good interviewer treats the prepared list as a starting point and spends most of the time on "tell me more about that" and "what happened next?" The most useful insight in a conversation often arrives in an aside, and a rigid script will talk right past it.

Third, listen for emotion. A flicker of irritation or a long sigh signals a problem that is real to the person, which is a better guide than anything they say they would like. Note it, and ask what lies behind it.

Fourth, resist the urge to pitch. The moment an interviewer starts describing a solution, the customer shifts from describing their world to evaluating yours. Save that for a separate conversation.

Finally, write things up promptly. Memory smooths the awkward details first, and those are the parts most worth keeping. A page of honest notes written the same afternoon beats an elegant summary written a week later.
