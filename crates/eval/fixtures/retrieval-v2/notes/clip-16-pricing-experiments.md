# Three pricing experiments that did not work

_Clipped from the web. Source: example.org/three-pricing-experiments-that-did-not-work._

Pricing is one of the few levers a business can pull in an afternoon, which is exactly why it is tempting to pull it carelessly. Founders and product managers who have tried it speak candidly about experiments that taught them more through failure than any success could have. Three stories came up repeatedly.

**The discount that trained customers to wait**

One team ran a sale each quarter to lift sign-ups. Numbers rose during the sale and fell sharply afterwards. Within a year, customers had learned the pattern and simply waited for the next discount. Revenue per customer slid, and the team spent months trying to persuade people that the regular price was fair. The lesson, in their words, was that a discount is a message about what the product is really worth.

**The price rise nobody explained**

Another team raised prices across the board with a short notice and no explanation. Complaints were modest, but quiet cancellations climbed in the following weeks. Exit conversations showed that most customers did not object to the amount so much as to the silence. A later increase, accompanied by a plain account of what had changed and what was coming, drew almost no reaction at all.

**The clever tiers**

The third team built a pricing page with five tiers and a careful matrix of features. Visitors lingered, then left. Sales conversations revealed that people could not tell which tier suited them, so they chose none. Reducing the page to three options, each described in terms of who it was for rather than what it contained, brought conversions back within weeks.

None of these stories proves a rule. Each team's circumstances differed, and each admits that other factors played a part. Still, the common thread is hard to miss: customers respond to clarity and consistency more than to cleverness. Pricing experiments tend to fail when they treat buyers as variables rather than people who are trying to make a sensible decision with limited time.

Several of those interviewed offered the same practical advice. Change one thing at a time, tell customers what you are doing, and decide beforehand what result would count as failure. Without that last step, almost any outcome can be rationalised afterwards.
