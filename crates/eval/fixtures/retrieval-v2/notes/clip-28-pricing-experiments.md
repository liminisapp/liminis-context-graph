# Three pricing experiments that did not work

Most pricing advice reads like a list of victories. The experiments that go wrong tend to be quietly dropped, which is a pity, because their failures are often more instructive than the successes. Here are three that sound sensible on paper and disappointed in practice.

**The deep discount to win attention.** The first experiment looked simple: cut the price sharply for a limited period and see whether volume rose enough to compensate. Volume did rise, but the customers who arrived were not the ones the business wanted. They compared the offer with every alternative, left at the first sign of a price increase, and generated more support requests than the regular customers did. When the original price returned, many of them left. The lesson drawn by the analysts was that a discount selects for people who care mostly about discounts, and that measuring only the surge in sign-ups hides what happens afterwards.

**The tiered plan with too many tiers.** The second experiment tried to serve every kind of buyer by offering a ladder of plans, each differing from the next in small ways. In theory, more choice means a closer fit. In practice, shoppers spent longer deciding and a larger share of them abandoned the purchase altogether. Those who did buy tended to pick the cheapest option out of caution. Interviews afterwards suggested that people did not know what they would need and found the differences between tiers hard to evaluate. Simplifying the ladder to a few clearly distinct options brought the abandonment rate down, although the team admitted that it could not separate the effect of the simpler plans from other changes made at the same time.

**The price rise without explanation.** The third experiment raised prices for existing customers and said as little as possible about it, on the theory that a long explanation invites objections. The silence backfired. Customers noticed the change anyway, and in the absence of a reason they supplied their own, usually less flattering than the truth. Cancellations ticked up in the weeks that followed, and so did complaints about trust. A later, smaller increase accompanied by a plain account of what had changed in costs and what the money would fund drew far less resistance.

What links the three is not that the ideas were foolish, but that each rested on an assumption nobody had tested: that new customers behave like existing ones, that choice is always welcome, and that silence is neutral. Careful practitioners suggest a few habits that help. Define in advance what success would look like beyond the first fortnight. Run the test long enough to see behaviour after the novelty fades. Talk to customers who left, not only those who stayed. And write down the assumptions before the experiment starts, so that when the result arrives there is something to compare it with.

None of this means experiments are a waste. It means the interesting part is often not the number on the dashboard but what the number is standing in for. A price is a message as well as a figure, and customers read it that way.
