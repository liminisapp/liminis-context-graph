# What small cooperatives get wrong about governance

Ask the members of a young cooperative what they like about it and the answers come quickly: the sense of ownership, the say over how money is spent, the absence of a distant boss. Ask them how decisions actually get made and the room tends to go quiet. Governance, the dull machinery that turns good intentions into durable practice, is where many small cooperatives quietly come unstuck.

The problem is rarely bad faith. More often it is the opposite. Founders who trust each other see no need to write things down, and for a while that works. Decisions happen in the kitchen, in a group chat, or at the end of a long meeting when half the members have already left. Everyone remembers the outcome slightly differently. Only when a newcomer asks why something is done a certain way does it become clear that no one can say.

**Mistaking consensus for governance**

The first common error is to treat consensus as a system in its own right. Seeking broad agreement is a fine habit, but it is not a procedure. Without a clear rule for what happens when agreement cannot be reached, the loudest or most persistent voice tends to win, and quieter members drift away. Researchers who study member-owned organisations often note that the groups that last have a fallback: a vote, a defined quorum, a named person who may break a tie. The fallback is rarely used. Its value is that everyone knows it exists, which makes people more willing to compromise.

**Roles that exist only in people's heads**

The second error is vagueness about who is responsible for what. In a small group, roles overlap, and that is natural. The trouble comes when a task has several loosely attached owners and therefore none. Treasurers who are not sure whether they approve spending or merely record it, secretaries who are unsure whether minutes should be circulated, and committees that nobody remembers establishing all follow from the same gap. Advisers suggest a one-page list of responsibilities, reviewed once a year, as the cheapest remedy available.

**Meetings without memory**

Third, many cooperatives meet often and remember little. Notes are taken but not stored where anyone can find them; actions are agreed but not tracked; and the same topic returns every few months as though it were new. A modest discipline helps. Record each decision in a sentence, with the date and the reason, and keep the records in one place that every member can open. The aim is not bureaucracy but continuity, so that a decision made by one set of members can be understood by the next.

**Forgetting the exit**

A fourth blind spot is the leaving of members. Founding documents usually say a good deal about joining and little about departing. When someone wants to leave, or is asked to, the absence of a clear process turns a manageable administrative matter into a personal dispute. Questions about what a departing member is owed, how long they remain responsible for commitments and how their role is covered are far easier to settle in advance than in the middle of a disagreement.

**Confusing size with simplicity**

Finally, small organisations often assume that their size excuses them from formality. A group of a dozen people, the thinking goes, can sort things out by talking. Often that is true, until it is not. Growth, a change in membership or a dispute over money can arrive without warning, and the habits that served a handful of friends are poorly suited to a group with divergent interests. Governance written when things are calm is much cheaper than governance improvised in a crisis.

**What helps**

None of this demands heavy machinery. Practitioners who work with small cooperatives tend to recommend the same short list: write down how decisions are made and what to do when agreement fails; name owners for recurring tasks; keep a single record of decisions; agree in advance how members leave; and review all of it on a regular schedule. The review matters most. Rules that are never revisited harden into ritual, and rituals that no one understands are the first thing to be ignored.

There is also a cultural point. Members sometimes fear that formal rules signal distrust. The better view is that clear rules protect relationships by removing the need to rely on memory and goodwill alone. A group that can say, calmly, "here is how we decide this" has more freedom to disagree openly, because the disagreement has somewhere to go.

Governance will never be the exciting part of a cooperative. But the groups that treat it as a shared craft, not an imposition from outside, tend to be the ones still thriving long after the founding enthusiasm has settled into routine.
