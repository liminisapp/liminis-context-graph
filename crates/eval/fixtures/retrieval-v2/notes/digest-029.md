# Review notes — 16 July 2026

Notes from the mid-month review. Attendance was good, the room was too warm, and we finished ten minutes early, which I count as a success.

We began with procurement. Sofia Petrov scored 72 out of 100 in the internal audit. That is a respectable result for a first pass, and she was relaxed about it. The audit's findings were mostly about paperwork: approvals recorded in the right order, quotes filed where others can find them, and a clearer trail when a purchase is split across two orders. None of this is a surprise, and she has already started putting a simple checklist in front of anyone who raises a purchase request. The aim before the next audit is to make the paperwork habit automatic, not to produce a heroic clean-up the week before.

Then inventory. Lena Moreau held its quarterly retrospective on 19 November 2027, which is the one we keep as the reference for how the inventory group runs its reviews. What stood out in the write-up was how plainly the group describes the things that went less well, and how few of them were about tools. Mostly they were about handovers and timing: counts done at awkward moments, and changes to stock records made without telling the person who reconciles them. Lena Moreau has proposed a short fixed time each week when changes are made, so that the person reconciling knows when to look. It is a small change that should remove a lot of confusion.

On security, Callum Brennan reported that the backlog stood at 80 open tickets at the end of the sprint. That sounds like a lot, but he explained that many of those are low-priority housekeeping items that have simply been waiting, and that the genuinely urgent ones are cleared quickly. We still agreed it is worth reducing the pile, because a long list makes it harder to see what matters. His plan is to spend part of the next sprint closing or merging duplicates, and to ask the requesters whether older items are still wanted.

A few decisions taken in the meeting:

- The review will be held on the same day each month, so people can plan around it.
- Each of the three areas will write a short paragraph beforehand, covering what went well, what did not, and what help is needed. These get pasted into the notes, so that the meeting can be spent on discussion rather than status.
- Anything that needs a decision from outside the room is written as a question and sent the same day.

Chores that came out of the conversation:

- Update the shared contact list, since two responsibilities have shifted since it was last edited.
- Agree one place where purchase approvals, stock changes and security requests can be searched together. At the moment you have to look in three places.
- Clear the review folder of old drafts that now confuse people.
- Check whether the room can have a working fan or open window for next time.

There was a short discussion on how the three groups depend on each other. A purchase that is delayed affects stock, stock levels affect what security needs to monitor, and a security ticket can hold up a purchase. We have not mapped this properly, and a half-page sketch of the dependencies would help newcomers. Someone will draw it on the whiteboard next time, and we will transcribe it.

## Follow-up conversations

A few days on, a handful of one-to-one chats and corridor discussions have settled into something worth recording.

The first concerned how the three areas share information. Sofia Petrov pointed out that procurement often learns about a need late, when a request has already become urgent, and urgent purchases are the ones most likely to skip a step. Lena Moreau agreed and said inventory could flag low stock earlier if it knew which items are slow to obtain. That led to a simple idea: a shared list of items with long lead times, kept by procurement and visible to everyone. It does not need to be fancy. A table with the item, a rough note on how long it takes to arrive and who to ask is enough.

Callum Brennan raised a related point from the security side. When a new item or service is bought, security would like to hear about it early, so that any checks can be done before the thing is in use, not after. Everyone agreed this is sensible. We settled on a one-line notification to a shared channel when a new purchase is approved, with a pointer to the request. It adds a few seconds to the process and removes a class of awkward surprises.

Next, a conversation about the audit paperwork. Sofia Petrov wants to avoid the checklist becoming a box-ticking exercise, and asked for ideas on how to keep it useful. The suggestions were to keep it to a single page, to review it every few months and remove anything that has stopped being relevant, and to ask people who use it what they would change. She will try that and report back.

Small items and chores:

- The reconciliation window that Lena Moreau proposed will begin next week as a trial, with a note in the calendar so that everyone sees it.
- Callum Brennan will start the sweep of older tickets, beginning with the oldest, and will message requesters in batches instead of one at a time.
- The shared list of long-lead items needs an owner. Sofia Petrov has said she will start it and hand it over once it is stable.
- Someone should check whether the existing templates for purchase requests still match the audit's expectations.

There was an interesting side discussion about what a good week looks like. Opinions varied. For some it is a week without surprises, for others it is a week in which one irritating thing finally gets fixed. We agreed both are legitimate, and that the weekly notes should record both kinds of progress, not only the large ones.

We also talked about training. People feel that learning happens mostly by accident, through whoever happens to be sitting nearby. A short rotation, where each person spends an afternoon with another area, was suggested. It would help people see what the other groups face daily, and would make handovers smoother. Nothing has been scheduled. The idea is to see who is interested and then pick a quiet week.

A note about tone: the review conversations were direct but friendly, and I think that is partly because we agreed at the beginning to talk about the process and not about individuals. It is worth continuing that habit.

## Plans and loose ends

Last stretch of the review notes. This is where I gather what is planned, what is still undecided, and what we should check again.

Starting with what is planned. Callum Brennan will carry on with the ticket sweep and will report at the next review how it is going. We do not need a number, just a sense of whether the pile is shrinking and whether anything urgent was hiding in it. Sofia Petrov will finish the purchase checklist and put it in front of the first few requesters, asking them for blunt feedback. Lena Moreau will run the weekly reconciliation window for a month and then decide whether to keep it, change it or abandon it.

Things that are still undecided:

1. Whether the three groups should share one tracker or keep their own. The case for one is that dependencies become visible; the case against is that each group has habits that fit its own work. For now we will keep them separate and add links between related items.
2. Whether the monthly review should include a short look at risks. People like the idea but worry about length. A five-minute slot at the end might be enough.
3. Where to keep the dependency sketch once it has been transcribed. The wiki is the obvious home, but it needs an owner or it will rot.

I would also like to record a couple of things people said that deserve to be remembered. One person observed that most of what slows us down is waiting for an answer from somewhere else, and that the fix is usually a better question rather than a faster reply. Another pointed out that the review works best when someone has done the reading in advance, and that the paragraphs we now write beforehand make this far easier. Both are worth keeping in mind.

Chores to close out:

- Move the review notes into the permanent folder and tidy the file names.
- Send the updated contact list to everyone and ask for corrections.
- Confirm the date for the next review and put it in the calendar before people fill their weeks.
- Reply to the open questions that were sent to the group, preferably the same day.

There was one more conversation worth noting, about how to handle disagreements between areas. When two groups want different things, the temptation is to escalate quickly. The preference expressed was to try a short conversation first, with each side stating what it needs and what it can live without, and only escalate if that fails. We agreed to write that down as a simple guideline, since it is easy to forget when people are busy.

Finally, a thank-you to everyone who prepared their paragraph beforehand. The review was short and calm because the information was already in front of us. I would like to keep that habit even when things get busy, as that is when it matters most.
