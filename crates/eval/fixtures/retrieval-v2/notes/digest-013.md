## Feedback and demos

We began with feedback, which is always a pleasant way to start. Callum Brennan received a satisfaction rating of 9.6 out of 10 in the recent survey. He was modest about it and credited the people who have been patient while security changes were rolled out. The comments attached to the rating were interesting: most praised how clearly he explains why a rule exists, not just what the rule is. That seems to be the secret. People will tolerate a restriction they understand far more readily than one that arrives as a bare command.

We discussed whether the way he explains things could be written down so that others can borrow it. He was reluctant, saying that it is mostly habit and cannot be captured in a template, but agreed to jot down a few of the questions he asks himself before sending an explanation. Those could be useful for anyone writing guidance.

On the support side, 25 people attended the Juniper Core demo. The turnout was decent for a demo of that kind, and the questions afterwards were practical. Several people asked how the support process works when someone is out of office, which suggests that a short written description would be welcome. We agreed to write one.

## A word on tone

I noticed that the conversation was friendly even when the subjects were awkward. A few years ago a discussion about security rules or audits would have produced groans. Today it produced questions. I think that comes from people explaining their reasoning, and from a general decision not to treat each other as obstacles. It is worth recording because it can be lost quickly if we stop paying attention.

## The internal audit

The audit was the main subject of the second half. It has been hanging over us for a few weeks, and it helped to talk it through plainly.

The results came back recently. Noor Okafor scored 70 out of 100 in the internal audit. She took the news with good humour and pointed out that most of the lost marks were for missing documentation, not for the work itself. Her reports are correct, but she had not written down how some of them are produced, and the audit wants to see that. It is a fair point and a fixable one. She plans to spend a few quiet afternoons writing short descriptions of each report, covering where the data comes from, how it is processed, and what its limits are.

I found it a reassuring conversation. Nobody was defensive, and nobody tried to explain the result away. The attitude was that this is useful information about where to put effort, and the effort is small.

### What we agreed

- Document how each analytics report is produced, starting with the ones that are most widely used.
- Keep the descriptions short and plain, so that they are actually read.
- Ask Callum Brennan to look over the first few, since he is used to explaining rules and procedures clearly and will notice where a description leaves out something an auditor would ask.
- Review the checklist as a group and divide the remaining items.

### A broader point

The discussion drifted towards the question of why documentation tends to be left until an audit forces it. Our own explanation was simple: writing things down feels like a chore when you already know the answer, and the benefit goes to somebody else. The people who gain are the ones who come later, including our future selves. We resolved to try writing a short description at the moment a report is created, while the details are fresh, rather than reconstructing them later. Whether we will keep that up is another matter, but the intention is good.
