# Blameless incident reviews in practice

When a system fails, the instinct in most organisations is to ask who was responsible. It is a natural question, and a deeply unhelpful one. Practitioners who study how failures unfold argue that the person nearest the problem is rarely its cause. More often they are the last link in a chain of decisions, shortcuts and unexamined assumptions that made the failure possible long before anyone typed the wrong command.

A blameless review starts from that premise. It does not mean that nobody is accountable, a misreading that critics frequently make. It means that the review treats individual mistakes as symptoms to be understood rather than offences to be punished. The question changes from "who did this?" to "how did our arrangements make this a reasonable thing to do?"

**Why blame backfires**

Where people expect punishment, they learn to hide. Details are softened, timelines are tidied, and the awkward fact that would have explained everything goes unmentioned. The organisation ends up with a neat account that is subtly wrong, and learns nothing from it. Researchers in safety-critical fields have documented the pattern for decades: the places that treat reporting as safe tend to hear about small problems early, while the places that treat it as risky hear about them only when they are no longer small.

There is a quieter cost as well. When a single person is named, the review tends to stop there. The fix becomes "be more careful", which is not a fix at all, because carefulness is not a control that anyone can switch on at will. The conditions that produced the error remain in place, waiting for the next person.

**What a good review looks like**

Most guides describe a similar shape. A facilitator, ideally someone who was not involved, gathers the people who were. The group builds a timeline from the evidence available, such as logs, messages and the recollections of those present, before anyone offers an explanation. Only once the sequence is agreed does the conversation move to why events happened as they did.

Useful questions tend to be open and concrete. What did the person know at that moment? What information was missing, and why? Which alarms were firing, and were any of them ignored because they fire constantly? What would have made the safer path the easier one? The tone matters as much as the content. A facilitator who says "walk me through what you saw" gets a different review from one who says "why didn't you check?"

**Turning findings into change**

A review that ends with a document and no action has largely failed. The strongest teams finish with a short list of changes, each with an owner and a date, and they revisit the list later. The changes are generally modest: a clearer warning, a confirmation step before a destructive operation, a runbook that matches reality, a rota that does not leave one person alone on a bad night.

Some teams also record what went well. Failures are usually contained by somebody doing something sensible under pressure, and that behaviour is worth noticing and repeating. A review that only dwells on what broke gives a lopsided picture of how the system actually works.

**Common pitfalls**

Observers note a handful of recurring problems. The first is the review that is blameless in name only, where the language is gentle but everyone still knows who is meant. The second is the review held too late, when memories have faded and the urgency has gone. The third is the review that produces so many actions that none is completed. Trimming the list to what will really be done is usually better than recording everything that could be done.

A fourth, subtler, is treating the practice as a ritual. Written templates are helpful, but a form completed to satisfy a process does little. The value lies in the conversation, and in the trust that makes it candid.

**A cultural commitment**

Ultimately, blameless reviews depend on what leaders do afterwards. If someone who admits a mistake is later passed over or quietly sidelined, word spreads quickly and candour disappears. If, instead, they are thanked for making a hard thing easier to understand, the next review will be better. The method is simple to describe and difficult to sustain, because it asks organisations to be most generous at precisely the moment they feel least inclined to be.
