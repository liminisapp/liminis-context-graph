# Review notes — 28 August 2025

Sprint review day. These are my notes, typed up on the train home, so the order follows my scribbles and not the agenda.

## Opening

The review began a few minutes late because the room booking had clashed with another group. We shuffled to the smaller room and sat closer together than anyone would have chosen, but it made for an oddly focused conversation. Nobody could easily drift off with a laptop.

The tone was calm. We have had a couple of reviews recently that turned into arguments about priorities, and this one avoided that by starting with what had actually shipped and only then moving on to what had not.

## Compliance queue

The headline from the compliance side was the queue. The Bram Tanaka backlog stood at 35 open tickets at the end of the sprint. People reacted differently to that. One view was that it is a reasonable number given what came in; another was that a few of the tickets have been sitting for a long time and are really just waiting on answers from outside. Bram Tanaka himself was matter-of-fact about it and pointed out that a chunk of the queue consists of items that cannot move until somebody else replies. That seems right, and it suggests that the raw count overstates the pressure.

What we agreed to do:

- Separate the tickets that are waiting on someone outside from the ones that are genuinely ready for work, so the real queue is visible.
- Add a short note to each stalled ticket saying who we are waiting on and since when.
- Look at the split again at the next review and see whether the "ready" pile is shrinking.

There was a side discussion about whether stalled tickets should be closed after a long silence. The consensus was no, not automatically, but they should be reviewed on a regular basis so that nothing is forgotten. A ticket left open indefinitely is a small lie; a ticket closed too early is a small betrayal. We would rather err on the side of keeping them and being honest about their state.

## Process remarks

A few smaller things came up while we were at it.

The handover between sprints was clunky. Several people said that they only learned what had been left unfinished by finding half-done work in the shared folder. We talked about a short end-of-sprint note, no more than a paragraph per person, listing what is open and what has been abandoned. It will not be perfect, but it should reduce the surprises.

There was also a brief conversation about estimates. Opinions ranged from "they are useful for planning" to "they are fiction we agree to tell each other". I sit somewhere in the middle. Estimates are useful as a way to start a conversation about what is hard, and less useful as promises. We did not change anything, but I think it helped to say it out loud.

- The retrospective is moved to the following week because several people are away.
- The room clash needs raising with whoever manages the bookings, so it does not happen again.

More to follow in the next part, which covers the onboarding side and the plans for the coming sprint.

## Onboarding and learning

The second half of the review was about onboarding, and I was glad to hear it, because it is an area that can easily get squeezed out when the sprint is busy.

Fable Core gave a brief update. The main message was that the material for new joiners is in better shape than it was, though still uneven. Some sections are clear and recent; others read as if they were written for a different team in a different year. Fable Core completed 11 hours of training this term, which was mentioned partly as a point of pride and partly as a reminder that learning time does not appear by magic. It had to be protected, and it was protected, and the result is that the people involved are more confident about explaining things to newcomers.

I want to record the reasoning here, because it will be easy to forget. The argument was that the best onboarding material is written by people who have recently been confused. Someone who has been in the team for years no longer remembers what is unclear. Someone who joined a few months ago does. So the training time doubles as a way of surfacing what needs fixing.

### What we will do about it

- Ask recent joiners to mark up the existing material with the places where they got stuck.
- Turn the most-asked questions into a short list near the top of the onboarding pages.
- Keep the learning time in the calendar and treat it as untouchable when possible, with a note whenever it has to be moved.

There was a gentle disagreement about whether learning time should be tied to specific topics or left open. One side said that structure keeps people from drifting; the other said that choice is what makes learning stick. I think both are right. We settled on a loose arrangement: people may choose their own topics but are asked to share one thing they learned in the next team meeting, so that the time yields something for everyone.

### Mentoring

A related point was mentoring. We have never formalised it, and for the most part it happens naturally. Still, we noticed that some newcomers end up with plenty of informal guidance while others get very little, mostly depending on who they happen to sit near. Bram Tanaka suggested a simple pairing at the start, with no obligations beyond a weekly chat. It is easy to set up and easy to drop if it does not work, which is the kind of change this team tends to accept.

### Questions that remain

- Who keeps the onboarding material up to date once the current round of fixes is done? An owner would help.
- How do we know whether any of this works? We could ask newcomers a few simple questions after a month, but we should avoid turning it into a survey that nobody wants to answer.
- Is there anything we can remove? The pile of material only ever seems to grow.

### Wrap-up

By the end I had the sense that the review had been useful without being heavy. Nobody was defensive, and the action items were small enough to be believable. That is the combination I would like to see more often.

The last part of these notes covers planning for next sprint and a few leftovers that did not fit anywhere else.

## Next sprint and leftovers

To close out the notes, here is what we said about the coming sprint, along with the odds and ends that did not fit earlier.

### Planning for the next sprint

The plan is intentionally modest. After a sprint where several items spilled over, there was little appetite for taking on more than we could finish. We agreed to carry forward the unfinished work, add only what is clearly urgent, and leave some slack for the unexpected. Slack was a recurring word in the conversation. A sprint with no spare room tends to fail on the first surprise, and we have had enough of those.

We also agreed that anything new should be paired with something removed. This is not a strict rule, but a habit of asking "what are we dropping to make room?" has a way of making priorities honest.

Bram Tanaka will lead the work of separating stalled tickets from ready ones. It is a small job, but it will reveal where the real pressure is, and it should make the next review conversation easier. He asked for a quiet half day to do it, and we agreed that nobody should interrupt him unless something is on fire.

Fable Core will continue refining the onboarding material and will share a short summary at the next meeting. The focus is the most commonly asked questions, which should be quick to improve and valuable to the next person who joins.

### Leftovers

- The sprint board needs a proper clean. I volunteered, since I was the one who complained.
- Someone should write down the way we decide what counts as urgent. Right now it seems to mean "whatever the loudest person says", which is not a policy.
- We should check that the shared calendar has the retrospective on it, moved to the following week.
- The end-of-sprint note for each person is to be tried once, then reviewed.

### A few reflections

I left the review feeling that the team is in decent shape. Nobody is burned out, nobody is hiding problems, and the numbers, while not perfect, are explained and understood. That counts for a lot. I used to think a good review meant good numbers. Now I think it means honest numbers and a clear sense of what to do about them.

There were also a few moments of humour that I should record, if only because they make the room feel better. Someone observed that the stalled tickets are less a backlog than a waiting room, and the image stuck. We now refer to the "waiting room" and the "ready pile", and I suspect those names will survive for a while.
