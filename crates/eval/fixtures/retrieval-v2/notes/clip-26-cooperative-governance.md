# What small cooperatives get wrong about governance

Cooperatives are built on a promise of shared ownership, and that promise is easy to make and hard to keep. Advisers who work with small member-owned organisations say that the problems rarely begin with bad intentions. They begin with informality, and with the assumption that goodwill is a substitute for structure.

## Confusing consensus with governance

Many small cooperatives start out making decisions by general agreement, which works well when there are a dozen members who know each other. As the membership grows, the habit persists, but the mechanism no longer fits. Decisions drag, the loudest voices dominate and quieter members stop attending. Advisers suggest writing down which decisions belong to the full membership, which to an elected board and which to day-to-day managers.

## Neglecting the board

A board is often treated as a formality, filled by whoever is willing. Yet it carries real responsibility for oversight. Cooperatives that rotate members through the board on staggered terms, and give each newcomer a short orientation, tend to avoid both stagnation and sudden loss of knowledge.

## Poor records and unclear roles

Minutes that are never circulated, votes that are not recorded and roles that overlap are the quiet causes of later disputes. When a disagreement arises, there is nothing to point to. A simple habit of recording decisions and who owns them removes much of the friction.

## Avoiding hard conversations

The most common failing is reluctance to address conflict, especially between friends. Clear procedures for raising concerns, agreed before they are needed, allow problems to be handled early, while they are still small.
