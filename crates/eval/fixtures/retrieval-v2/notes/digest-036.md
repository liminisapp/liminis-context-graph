# Review notes — 8 July 2026

Midsummer review, written on a hot afternoon with the fans going and the windows propped open. Attendance was thinner than usual because of holidays, but the people who were there covered the ground.

## Training and capacity

The first item was training, since the term is nearly over and we wanted to see where things stand. Quill Core completed 5 hours of training this term, which is lower than anyone intended and which the group put down to a busy spring more than any lack of interest. Rather than treat it as a failing, the team talked about how to make learning easier to fit in. The main idea was to protect a short block in the calendar, so that training is not the first thing to be squeezed when work piles up. There was also a suggestion to favour short, focused sessions over long courses, because they are far easier to attend.

Marcus Haddad said telemetry has a similar problem: plenty of interest, not enough free hours. He is going to pick one topic that would help the team most and aim for that, rather than spreading himself thinly across several. That seemed sensible, and the team agreed to hold him to it, kindly.

## Routing work

Quill Core's other items were more routine. The simplification of old rules continues at a steady pace, and a few have been retired after checking that nothing depended on them. The team prefers to remove one thing at a time and watch what happens, which is slower but means that when something does break, the cause is obvious. A short note is kept alongside each removal so that anyone wondering later can see what was done and why.

## Telemetry work

Marcus Haddad also talked about the dashboards. Some were built for a specific question months ago and have not been looked at since. The approach is to ask each owner whether they still want theirs, and to archive the ones nobody claims. People are generally relaxed about this, as long as archived means recoverable.

## A thought on pace

It was observed during the review that we tend to set ambitious plans at the start of a term and then feel faintly guilty when they do not all happen. A more useful habit might be to plan a little less, finish most of it, and use the spare time for the things that crop up anyway. Nobody disagreed. Whether we will actually do it is another matter, but it is worth saying out loud.

The next review is scheduled for early autumn, in the same room. Bring a fan.

## Process and tooling

A slightly dry section, but a useful one. The team spent a while on how change requests move through routing and telemetry. At present there is an informal convention that anything risky gets a second pair of eyes, but nobody has written down what counts as risky. The result is inconsistency: some changes get over-reviewed, while others slip through because they looked harmless. The working definition proposed was anything that affects what other people see or rely on. It is imperfect, but it gives us a place to start and can be tightened as examples come up.

We also looked at how to record why a change was made. Commit messages tell you what changed; they rarely say why. A short "reason" line in the tracker entry would solve most of it, and the team agreed to try that for a couple of months. If it turns out to be a chore, we will reconsider. If it turns out to be valuable, it becomes the norm.

## Customers and requests

Quill Core mentioned a few requests that had come in from outside the usual channels. They were handled, but the way they arrived was awkward, and the group wants to make it clearer how people should ask. A short page with a form and a couple of examples is the plan. Marcus Haddad offered to review the wording from the telemetry side, since some of the requests overlap with data questions.

A recurring theme was expectations. People ask for things without knowing how long they take, and the team is often too polite to say. The proposal is to include a rough turnaround in the acknowledgement message, with a sentence on what could make it slower. It is more honest and, in the long run, kinder.

## Observations from the room

It struck me during the discussion how much of the team's friction comes from assumptions that are never stated. Someone assumes a message was seen; someone assumes a task was handed over. The antidote is not more meetings but more explicit acknowledgements: "got it", "I have this", "I cannot do this one". These cost nothing and make a big difference to how the work feels.

There was a related remark about tone in written messages. Short replies can come across as curt even when they are not meant that way. The group agreed that adding a word of context, even just "thanks, that helps", makes things easier to read, especially for people who are new or who work remotely.

## Offsite and next steps

Looking further ahead, the Marcus Haddad offsite will take place in Ghent, and the telemetry group has started thinking about what to do with the time. There is no appetite for a long slide-driven agenda. The preference is for a couple of working sessions on real problems, with plenty of room around them. One idea that got a warm response was to bring along an unsolved question from each person's work and spend an hour on it together, with the rule that no one is allowed to defend the current approach. It sounds mildly uncomfortable, which is probably a good sign.

Open questions on the offsite: how long it should run, whether partners from other groups should be invited for one session, and how to handle people who cannot travel. None needs an answer today. The next step is to write the options on one page and let people comment.

## Things we learned

Reflecting on the session, a couple of themes stand out. First, the team keeps rediscovering that small, regular habits beat big plans. The things that actually stuck from previous reviews were modest: a rota, a template, a weekly note. The things that did not stick were ambitious and needed everyone to change at once. Second, people are generally willing to try something if they know when it will be reviewed. The promise of a checkpoint makes experimentation feel safe.

## Housekeeping

The shared drive structure was discussed again, briefly. We still do not agree on whether folders should be organised by project or by type of document. The decision, for now, is to leave it alone, because the cost of reorganising exceeds the benefit. Search works well enough, and as long as files have sensible names, nobody gets lost.

A last reminder about the calendar: please mark leave early. Several plans this summer have been complicated by people discovering clashes late, and it is much easier to fix those when there is time.
