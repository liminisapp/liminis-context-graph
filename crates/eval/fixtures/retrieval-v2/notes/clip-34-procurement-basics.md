# A plain-language guide to purchasing for startups

Buying things is rarely the part of a young company that founders enjoy. It lacks the drama of fundraising and the satisfaction of shipping a product, and it tends to arrive as a series of small irritations: a subscription that renews without warning, a laptop order that stalls, an invoice nobody remembers approving. Yet purchasing is where a surprising amount of money quietly leaves the business, and where a few simple habits can save both cash and goodwill.

## Start with the need, not the catalogue

The most common mistake is to begin with a supplier's brochure instead of a clear statement of what the company actually requires. A short written description of the need, covering what the item or service must do, who will use it, and what happens if it fails, makes every later decision easier. It also gives the team something to point to when a salesperson steers the conversation toward a more expensive option.

Experienced buyers suggest separating "must have" from "nice to have" before any conversation starts. A must-have is something the business cannot function without; a nice-to-have is something that would be pleasant. Many costly contracts are built on the second category dressed up as the first.

## Compare more than the sticker price

Price matters, but it is only one line in the comparison. Delivery times, support arrangements, flexibility to cancel and the cost of switching away later all belong in the picture. A cheap offer that locks a company in for a long period can turn out to be the most expensive choice, particularly if the business changes direction.

A simple table helps. List the options in columns and the criteria in rows, and score each by hand. The exercise is rough, but it forces the team to say out loud what they value, and it exposes disagreements early, before money has changed hands.

Terms deserve a careful read. Automatic renewals, minimum commitments and notice periods are the clauses most likely to cause regret. If a clause is unclear, ask for it to be explained in plain words and, ideally, in writing. A supplier who is reluctant to do so has told you something useful.

## Build a light approval habit

Large companies have layers of sign-off. Startups cannot afford the delay, but they also cannot afford to let every person buy whatever they like. A workable middle path is a short rule: small, routine purchases can be made by anyone on the team, while larger or recurring ones need a second pair of eyes. The threshold should be set by the founders and written down where everyone can find it.

The aim is not control for its own sake. It is visibility. When one person knows about every recurring commitment, duplicates are caught, forgotten subscriptions are cancelled and surprises at the end of the month become rare.

## Keep a record of what you buy

A shared list of every recurring service, with its owner, its purpose and its renewal date, is one of the cheapest forms of financial hygiene available. It takes an afternoon to build and a few minutes a month to maintain. The owner column matters most: a service with no owner is a service nobody will question.

Receipts and contracts should live in a single, predictable place. Accountants and auditors will thank you, and so will the future colleague who has to work out what a mysterious monthly charge is for.

## Negotiate, politely

Many founders assume prices are fixed. Often they are not. Suppliers generally expect some conversation about terms, particularly for new customers who might grow. Asking about a discount for annual payment, a trial period or a gentler cancellation clause costs nothing. The worst answer is "no", and the tone of the reply can reveal how a supplier is likely to behave once something goes wrong.

It helps to have an alternative in hand. A buyer who can credibly walk away negotiates from a stronger position than one who has already decided. That does not mean playing suppliers against each other; it means knowing what else is out there.

## Relationships matter

Over time, the suppliers that matter most are the ones that answer questions quickly, admit mistakes and tell you early when something will be late. These qualities are hard to read from a quote, so it is worth asking other small companies about their experience. A single honest reference is worth more than a page of marketing.

Treat suppliers as partners wherever it is sensible. Pay on time, give clear instructions and be upfront about changes in plan. A supplier who feels respected will often go the extra mile when a deadline slips.

## When things go wrong

Delays, defects and billing errors happen to everyone. The best response is calm and documented. Write down what was agreed, what arrived and what the gap is, then raise it with the supplier in writing. Keep the tone factual. Most disputes resolve quickly when both sides can see the same facts.

If a problem repeats, take it as information. A one-off slip is human; a pattern is a reason to look elsewhere, and having a record makes that decision straightforward.

## A short checklist

- Write the need in a few lines before you shop.
- Separate must-haves from nice-to-haves.
- Compare terms, not just price.
- Set a simple approval rule and write it down.
- Keep one list of recurring services with owners and renewal dates.
- Ask for what you want in negotiation; politely, and in writing.
- Review suppliers regularly, and be willing to move on.

None of this is glamorous, and none of it requires specialist training. It requires attention, a little discipline and the willingness to ask questions that feel slightly awkward. For a young company, that combination is often enough to keep purchasing from becoming a quiet source of waste.
