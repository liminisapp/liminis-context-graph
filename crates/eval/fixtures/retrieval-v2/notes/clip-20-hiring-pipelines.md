# Building a hiring pipeline without a recruiter

For many small organisations, hiring is a task that arrives all at once and with no dedicated owner. A vacancy opens, someone is asked to deal with it on top of their normal work, and the process is invented from scratch. Hiring managers who have been through this describe the same pattern: a flurry of activity, a messy pile of applications and a nagging sense that good candidates have slipped through.

A pipeline does not require a specialist. It requires a sequence of steps that is written down, a person responsible for each stage and a habit of recording what happened. Those who have done it well say the discipline matters more than the tooling.

## Start with the role, not the advertisement

The first mistake is rushing to publish a job advertisement before the role is clear. Experienced managers recommend writing a short description of what the person will actually do in their first six months, and what success would look like. That text becomes the basis for the advertisement, the screening criteria and the interview questions. Without it, each stage is judged against a different, unspoken idea of what the job is.

It also helps to separate essentials from preferences. A list of fifteen requirements discourages capable applicants, particularly those who tend to apply only when they meet every one. Limiting the list to the few that genuinely cannot be learned on the job widens the field.

## Make screening consistent

Once applications arrive, the temptation is to skim them and trust instinct. Instinct is unreliable, and tends to favour candidates who resemble the person doing the reading. A more dependable approach is to agree a small set of criteria in advance, score every application against them and keep brief notes. Two readers scoring independently, then comparing, catches many errors.

A short written exercise or a structured screening call can replace the unstructured chat that many small teams default to. The aim is to ask every candidate the same core questions, so that differences in answers reflect differences between people rather than differences in the questioner's mood.

## Keep candidates informed

A recurring complaint from job seekers is silence. Applications that vanish without a reply damage an organisation's reputation, even if it never intended to be discourteous. Without an automated system, a simple shared sheet can track where each candidate stands and when they were last contacted. A rule such as replying to everyone within a set number of working days, even if the reply is a polite decline, costs little and pays back in goodwill.

Timelines matter as well. Strong candidates rarely wait long. If the process takes weeks between stages, those with other options will take them. Managers who have shortened their cycles say the biggest gains came from scheduling interviews in blocks and agreeing decisions the same day.

## Interviews that tell you something

Structured interviews, with a fixed set of questions and a shared scoring guide, consistently outperform free-form conversations at predicting how someone will do the job. They also make it easier to defend a decision. Panel members should write their scores before discussing, to avoid being swayed by the most confident voice in the room.

Practical exercises that resemble real work are also valued, provided they are short and respectful of the candidate's time. A task that takes an evening is a reasonable ask; one that takes a weekend is not, and it screens out people with caring responsibilities.

## Close well

The final stage is often handled hastily. Offers should be made promptly, in conversation first and in writing afterwards, with clear terms and a realistic start date. Declined candidates who were close deserve a personal note; they may be the right fit for a future role.

Finally, review the process. A brief look back at what worked, how long it took and where candidates dropped out will improve the next round. Over time, an informal scramble becomes a reliable routine, and the organisation finds that it needs a recruiter far less than it feared.
