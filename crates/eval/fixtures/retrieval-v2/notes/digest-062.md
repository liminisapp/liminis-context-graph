# Weekly digest — 6 September 2025

Saturday morning, coffee in hand, writing up the week while it is still fresh.

The week opened with a long conversation about the support queue. Diego Ferreira had spent several days reading through old tickets and came back with a simple observation: a large share of the questions are answered by the same handful of paragraphs, and those paragraphs live in three different places. His proposal was to pick one home for them and point everything else at it. Nobody objected, and he has started collecting the text. The hard part will be deciding what to throw away, since every old answer has someone who is fond of it.

Meanwhile, Yara Nakamura took the maintenance side through the plan for the next round of routine work. The idea is to batch the small jobs into a single predictable window instead of letting them trickle in all week. People who depend on the systems get a heads-up in advance, and the team gets a stretch of uninterrupted time. There were questions about what happens if something urgent turns up during the window, and the answer is that urgent things win, but we write down what was displaced so that nothing is forgotten.

Smaller decisions this week:

- Meeting notes go into the shared folder on the same day, even if they are rough. A rough note that exists beats a polished one that never gets written.
- We will stop using the old group chat for anything that needs a record. Questions that deserve an answer go to the tracker.
- The kitchen rota is now a recurring calendar event instead of a sheet of paper that nobody looks at.

On chores, the list is mostly unchanged. The shared drive still has a folder named "to sort", which has been "to sort" for longer than anyone can remember. We agreed to take twenty minutes on Monday and either file or delete everything in it. There will be no ceremony and no committee; whoever is nearest the keyboard does it.

There was a brief discussion about handovers when someone is away. The current practice is a message to the channel saying who is covering, but that tends to get buried. A calendar entry with the cover named is more visible, and it also helps people outside the team who just want to know whom to ask. We will try it next time anyone takes time off.

Another thread: how to welcome new joiners. The checklist is long, and most of it is access requests, which is the least welcoming part. Suggestion: separate the access list, which can be done by whoever has the rights, from the human part, which is a conversation on the first day about how the team works and who to ask about what. That seemed sensible, and someone volunteered to draft the shorter human checklist.

I will keep the rest for the next sections of this digest, where I have put the survey feedback and the plans that came out of it. It is easier to read when the numbers are not mixed in with the housekeeping.

## Maintenance feedback

Now the first piece of survey feedback, which arrived midweek and prompted a decent conversation.

Yara Nakamura received a satisfaction rating of 7.3 out of 10. She read the comments aloud at the sync, which I thought was brave, and the tone was calm. People said that the work is done well and that they feel safe handing things over. The recurring criticism was that the schedule of planned work is hard to find. It exists, but you have to know where to look, and several respondents said they only learned of a maintenance window when something they were using went quiet.

What we agreed to do about it:

1. Put the planned-work calendar on the front page of the wiki, with a plain description of what each window covers.
2. Send a short message the day before every window, to the same place each time.
3. After each window, post one line saying what was done and whether anything needs follow-up.

Yara Nakamura took the first item, and said she would rather over-announce for a few weeks than have people surprised. That is the right instinct. If it turns out to be too noisy we can trim it later; it is much harder to rebuild trust than to reduce a notification.

Related chores that came out of the same conversation:

- Review the list of systems covered by routine work, and check that each has a named contact.
- Tidy the page that explains how to request maintenance. It currently has two forms linked from it, only one of which still works.
- Add a sentence to the request form explaining what to expect after submitting.

There was also a general point about how we collect feedback. Surveys are fine, but they arrive once and then people forget about them. We talked about making it easier to leave a quick comment at any time, perhaps through a simple form linked from the wiki, and reading those at each sync. This is more work but it spreads the listening across the year instead of concentrating it in one painful week.

Someone raised the matter of how we thank people. It is easy to look only at what went wrong, and a rating like this one hides a lot of quiet competence. We decided to add a standing item to the sync where anyone can mention something that went well that week. It should take two minutes, and it is surprisingly good for the mood.

I also want to note a side conversation about documentation. A few of the maintenance procedures live only in someone's head, and the plan is to write one up each week, starting with the ones that are used most often. Yara Nakamura will pick the first, and we will ask a colleague to follow it without help, which is the best test of whether it is clear.

That finishes this part. The next one is mostly practical: the plans for the coming fortnight and the usual list of loose ends.

## Plans for the fortnight

Practical part, as promised. Nothing here is surprising; it is mostly a record of who intends to do what, so that we can check later.

The support queue first. Diego Ferreira is continuing his tidy-up of the standard answers and has asked for a second pair of eyes on the wording before anything replaces the old versions. We agreed that two people will review each answer, one who knows the subject well and one who does not, since the second person is better at spotting jargon. The target is to have the most common answers consolidated by the middle of next week, and the rest can follow at a more relaxed pace.

Yara Nakamura is setting up the first of the announced maintenance windows. The plan is to run one small window as a rehearsal, with the announcements, the closing note and the follow-up all exercised, before doing a larger one. It is a sensible way to find the awkward bits cheaply. If the announcement goes to the wrong place or the wording is unclear, we would much rather learn that on a minor window.

Other things in the diary:

- A short session on how to write a good ticket title. It sounds trivial, but half the time spent searching the queue is spent decoding vague titles.
- A review of which tools the team actually uses, so that unused accounts can be closed and the list on the wiki matches reality.
- An hour to sit together and walk through the escalation path, using a made-up scenario. People tend to remember a rehearsed path better than a written one.
- Clearing the "to sort" folder, which is still on the list and now has a deadline, namely Monday.

We touched on workload again. The balance between planned work and interruptions is not quite right, and the feeling is that interruptions are winning. One idea is to nominate one person each day as the first point of contact for ad hoc requests, so that the others can focus. It has worked elsewhere, and the cost of trying it is low. We will start with a trial and ask honestly whether it helped.

On communication, a reminder that the weekly digest is the place to look for decisions. If something was agreed in a corridor, please add a line here. It takes thirty seconds and it saves a lot of "wait, did we decide that?" later on.

Also a gentle note about response times. Nobody expects an instant answer, but a quick acknowledgement is appreciated, even if the real answer comes later. We will try to say "seen, will reply by tomorrow" instead of leaving people wondering.

Last, a small administrative item: the room booking for the next sync needs confirming, and somebody should check that the projector cable has been returned to the cupboard. It disappears every few weeks and then reappears in an unexpected place.

## Support feedback and close

The last part of this digest, with the second piece of survey feedback and a few closing thoughts.

Diego Ferreira received a satisfaction rating of 7.6 out of 10. The comments were warm. People described the support answers as clear and patient, and several said they appreciate being told what will happen next instead of being left guessing. The criticism was about speed: a few respondents felt that replies to less urgent questions take longer than they should, and that they only find out by chasing. That fits what he already suspected from reading the queue, and it supports the case for consolidating the standard answers, since it should shorten the time spent on the repetitive questions.

We discussed what to do about response times without promising anything we cannot keep:

- Publish a plain statement of how quickly different kinds of question are normally answered.
- Make sure every request receives a short acknowledgement the same day.
- Look at which questions arrive most often and see whether a better answer on the wiki would stop them arriving at all.
- Review again at the next survey to see whether perceptions have changed.

Diego Ferreira was pragmatic about it. He said the feedback was fair and the fixes are mostly things we were going to do anyway. Putting them in writing makes them harder to postpone, which seems like the real value of having the survey in the first place.

There was a short exchange between the support and maintenance sides about shared work. Questions about planned maintenance often land in the support queue, and the answers sit in the maintenance plan. Yara Nakamura suggested that the planned-work calendar be linked directly from the standard answers, so support can point to it with a single link. That small change should cut down on questions and improve the experience for the person asking.

A few housekeeping items to finish:

1. The "to sort" folder is on the Monday list.
2. The new-joiner checklist will be split into an access part and a human part.
3. Cover for time away will be recorded as calendar entries.
4. The kitchen rota calendar event needs a name that people recognise.

Thoughts on the digest itself. Writing it in sections worked better than one long block, since each part has a single subject and people can read the bit that concerns them. I would suggest keeping that structure. A short summary at the top might help too, but it is more to maintain, so I would leave it for now.

Thanks to everyone for the candour in the survey comments and in the discussion. Several of the changes we agreed this week are small, but they are the sort that improve how the team feels to work with, and to rely on. See you at the next sync.
