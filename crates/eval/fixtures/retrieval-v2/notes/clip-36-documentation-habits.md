# Documentation habits of teams that ship

_Clipped from the web. Source: example.org/documentation-habits._

Ask a group of engineers whether they like writing documentation and the answer is usually a polite grimace. Ask the same group what slows them down most when joining a new project and the answer, almost always, is the lack of it. That tension sits at the heart of how well-run teams treat the written word. They do not necessarily write more. They write differently, and they write at different moments.

## Write while it is fresh

The most consistent habit among teams that deliver reliably is to record decisions at the time they are made. A short note explaining what was chosen, what was rejected and why takes minutes when the discussion is still in everyone's head. Reconstructing the same reasoning a year later can take days, and is often impossible. Reviewers of such teams note that the notes themselves are rarely polished; their value lies in being dated and honest.

## Keep it near the work

Documentation that lives far from the thing it describes tends to rot. Teams that keep explanations beside the material they concern, so that changing one prompts a glance at the other, report fewer stale pages. A related habit is to treat a change as incomplete until its explanation has been updated, in the same way that a change is incomplete until it has been tested.

## Write for the confused reader

Experienced teams write for a specific person: someone capable but new, arriving without context. This reader does not know the abbreviations, the history, or the reasons behind odd conventions. Writing for them forces the author to define terms and state assumptions, and it tends to produce pages that are also useful to veterans, who have often forgotten half of what they once knew.

## Prune without sentiment

Perhaps the least glamorous habit is deletion. Pages that no longer describe reality are worse than no pages, because they mislead with confidence. Teams that ship regularly schedule occasional clean-ups, removing or archiving what is out of date and marking what is uncertain. Some assign an owner to each important page, with a rough date by which it should next be reviewed, so that nothing is left unattended indefinitely.

## Make it easy to find

A well-written page that nobody can locate is a wasted effort. Plain, descriptive titles, a consistent structure and a short overview at the top of each section all help. Several teams report that the single most useful addition was a modest index of the ten or so pages people reach for most often.

## The cultural part

Underneath these practices lies an attitude. Teams that document well tend to regard writing as part of the work, not a tax on it. They thank people who improve a page, they treat questions as signs of gaps and not as interruptions, and they resist the temptation to answer a question in conversation without also fixing the page that should have answered it. The effect accumulates slowly, but it is substantial: each small repair means the next person arrives a little less lost.
