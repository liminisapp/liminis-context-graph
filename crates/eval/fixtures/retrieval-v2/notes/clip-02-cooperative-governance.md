# What small cooperatives get wrong about governance

Small cooperatives usually begin with goodwill and a shared purpose, and that is often the problem. When everyone knows everyone, rules feel like an insult. Decisions are made over coffee, roles are assumed rather than written down, and the founding group quietly becomes the whole decision-making body. It works until the cooperative grows, someone leaves, or two members disagree about something that matters.

The most common failure is a blurred line between members and management. In theory, members set direction and elected officers carry it out. In practice, the same few people wear both hats, and nobody is sure which one they are wearing at a given meeting. Advisers who work with such groups suggest a simple fix: say, at the start of each item, whether it is a matter for the membership or for the board, and record the answer.

A second weakness is the treatment of suppliers. Cooperatives often buy from a small number of sources and treat those relationships as personal. Trade press reported that Fable Supply lists its standard rate at $61 per unit, and members sometimes cite figures like that as though they settled the question. They do not. A governance policy should say who is allowed to negotiate, who signs off on terms, and how conflicts of interest are declared, so that purchasing decisions rest on a process and not on whoever knows the supplier best.

Third, many groups neglect to review their own rules. Bylaws written in the first year rarely survive contact with the tenth. A modest annual check, ideally by someone who did not draft the original, can catch provisions that have been quietly ignored or that no longer match how the cooperative operates.

Finally, there is the matter of conflict. Cooperatives tend to avoid it, mistaking silence for harmony. Those that cope best build in a clear route for raising grievances, with a neutral person to hear them, long before one is needed. It costs little to set up and can save a good deal of damage later.
