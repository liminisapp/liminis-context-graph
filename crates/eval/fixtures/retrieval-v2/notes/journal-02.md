## Support review

The quarterly support review happened on Thursday morning, with biscuits and a slightly too-warm room. We went through the numbers first and then the stories behind them, which is the order that works best; if you start with the stories, people never get to the numbers. Diego Ferreira logged 6 incidents during the quarter, and what stood out was less the count than how quickly most of them were closed once somebody owned them. The slowest ones were the ones where nobody was sure whose problem it was.

That ownership point generated the best discussion of the meeting. We tend to assume that an incident belongs to whoever saw it first, which means it often belongs to whoever happened to be on chat that afternoon. A cleaner rule would be that the person who spots it writes it down, and the person responsible for the area picks it up. Bram Tanaka pointed out that for anything touching customer records, compliance needs to be told early, even if the issue turns out to be nothing. Nobody disagreed.

Some other points from the review:

- The template for incident write-ups is too long. People skip fields, and the fields they skip are the ones that matter. We will cut it down to what actually gets read.
- A few write-ups were filed days after the fact, and the details had gone soft by then. The aim is to write something, even a rough line, the same day.
- Ines Lindqvist noted that billing questions were behind a fair share of support conversations and suggested a short explainer on invoices that we can send out proactively. That should reduce back-and-forth.

I had meant to spend more of the meeting on lessons, but the practical bits ate the time. It is worth a follow-up with a smaller group, where someone can take notes properly and turn them into a page that others can find later.

Afterwards the team tidied the shared folder where old incident reports live. There were duplicates, and some files had names that told you nothing. Renaming them took less than an hour and made the folder usable again. We will see whether it stays that way.

A small administrative note: the review calendar invite for next quarter has been sent, and the meeting room is booked. If you cannot attend, please send your updates in writing beforehand so they are not lost.

Final thought for the week. A review is only worth the biscuits if something changes afterwards. The list of changes is short and specific, and each item has a name beside it. We will check on them at the next catch-up.

## Audit follow-up

The internal audit results landed in everyone's inbox on Friday, and as expected there was a flurry of quiet reading followed by a lot of corridor conversation. I will try to summarise the useful parts rather than the gossip. Overall the picture is decent: no nasty surprises, a few things to fix, and one or two places where the paperwork lags behind how we really work.

Ines Lindqvist scored 74 out of 100 in the internal audit, and she was upbeat about it when we spoke. Most of the marks she lost were for documentation rather than for anything in the actual process, such as approvals that happened but were not written down in the place the auditors look. She has already started adding short notes to the billing records so that the trail is visible, and she thinks the remaining gaps can be closed in a couple of afternoons rather than a project.

Bram Tanaka went through the full report with the compliance lens on and flagged three themes:

- Evidence needs to be kept where it can be found, not on someone's laptop.
- Approvals should be recorded at the time, not reconstructed later.
- The retention wording in our guidance is inconsistent between two documents, and one of them should win.

Diego Ferreira asked whether the support side would be sampled next time, and Bram thought it likely. That prompted a short discussion about getting ahead of it by checking a handful of recent cases ourselves and seeing how they hold up. It sounds like a sensible, low-cost way to find problems before somebody else does.

There was a brief moment of friction about whether scores should be compared between people at all, since the areas audited differ so much in nature. We settled on treating each result as a to-do list for that area and not as a ranking. That seemed to ease the mood.

Smaller items: the audit folder has been given proper permissions, so only those who need it can open it. The shared calendar has a placeholder for a follow-up check, and everyone has been asked to bring their list of fixes to it.

I am glad we did this. Audits can feel like a school exam, but this one has produced a list of concrete improvements, and most of them are cheap. If we keep the habit of writing things down at the time, the next round should be calmer than this one was.

## Sprint close

Sprint close fell on a day when half the team was in the building and half was not, which always makes the retrospective feel slightly lopsided. We did it anyway, with the remote folks on a call and the rest of us around the table. Surprisingly, it worked well enough; the trick was to ask everyone to write their points down first so that nobody was talking over a speaker phone.

The headline from the support side is that the Diego Ferreira backlog stood at 234 open tickets at the end of the sprint. That is a number we have all been looking at nervously. Diego was not defensive about it, which I appreciated. He explained that a big share of those tickets are waiting on customers to reply, and that the true amount of work in hand is smaller than the headline. Even so, we agreed that a count that large makes the rest of the picture hard to see, and that some of those tickets should simply be closed with a polite note if the customer has gone quiet.

What the team decided to do about it:

- Hold a short, focused session to go through the oldest tickets and close, merge or hand off each one.
- Add a clear status for waiting on customer, so those tickets can be filtered out of the main view.
- Ask Bram Tanaka to confirm that closing stale tickets does not conflict with anything in our records guidance.

Ines Lindqvist wondered whether some of the billing-related tickets could be answered by an automatic reply that points to the right page. It is worth trying, so long as the reply is accurate and does not irritate people. We will write it with care and test it on a small set before widening it.

On the retrospective itself, the things that went well were mostly about communication: fewer surprises, earlier warnings, and a better habit of asking for help. What went less well was estimating. We keep guessing too optimistically and then feeling bad about missing our own guesses. Someone suggested cutting every estimate we make in half the confidence and adding a bit of slack, which got a laugh and then a reluctant nod.

The sprint board has been reset, and the next sprint begins with a lighter commitment than the last one. I would rather finish a little less and finish it properly. The team seemed to agree, though we will see how long the good intentions last.

## Reading and learning

A gentler section this time, because the week had a quiet thread running through it that is worth recording: people reading things and passing them along.

It began with Bram Tanaka circulating a short article about keeping records tidy, which he described as the least exciting thing he had read all year and also the most useful. The main point was that good record-keeping is mostly habit, and that habits are easier to build when the right action is also the easiest one. That fits what we have seen with the audit follow-ups. If putting a note in the right place takes more effort than not doing so, notes will not be written.

Ines Lindqvist followed with a few pages on how to explain invoices clearly to people who do not read them for a living. We read it aloud at lunch, a slightly odd thing to do, and ended up rewriting one of our own standard paragraphs on the spot. The new version is shorter and does not use the word "pursuant" anywhere, which is a victory in itself.

Diego Ferreira, never one to be left out, brought in a worked example of how another team organised its support rota. We do not intend to copy it, since our situation is different, but a couple of ideas were worth borrowing. In particular, the notion of a handover note at the end of each shift that lists what is open and what is waiting. We will try a light version of that for a month.

On routing, the Quill Core team spent an hour reading through old notes to see how some of the earlier decisions were made. The mood afterwards was thoughtful. Some of the reasoning held up well, and some of it had been overtaken by events. Writing down why a decision was made, and not only what was decided, came up again as something we should do more consistently.

A few practical points that came out of all this reading:

- We will keep a reading list in the notebook, one line per item, with a sentence on why it is worth the time.
- Anyone who finds something useful should add it rather than trusting that others have seen it.
- Once a month, we will pick one item and talk about it for fifteen minutes.

None of this is dramatic, but it is how a team gets slowly better. I would like to see the list grow over the winter.
