# Documentation habits of teams that ship

_Clipped from the web. Source: example.org/documentation-habits-of-teams-that-ship._

Ask engineers what slows a project down and documentation rarely tops the list. Ask the people who joined a project late, or who inherited one, and the answer changes quickly. In interviews with managers and individual contributors across a range of organisations, a pattern emerged: the teams that deliver reliably are not necessarily the ones that write the most. They are the ones that write the right things, in the right place, at the right moment.

**Write for the reader who was not there**

The most consistent habit was also the simplest. Notes were written for someone who had missed the meeting. That meant stating the decision before the discussion, naming who was responsible for what, and giving a rough date for anything that would happen next. Several managers described a test they apply before filing anything: could a colleague on leave for two weeks read this and know what to do on their return? If the answer is no, the note goes back for another pass.

This sounds obvious, yet it runs against the grain of how most meeting notes are produced. The default is a transcript of who said what, in order. Transcripts are easy to write and tiresome to read. The better alternative puts conclusions first and treats the discussion as optional detail for those who want to follow the reasoning.

**Keep documents close to the work**

A second habit was proximity. Documentation that lives far from the code, the tickets or the tools tends to rot. Teams that ship consistently keep their notes next to the thing they describe: a short readme beside the code, a decision record attached to the relevant ticket, a runbook linked directly from the alert that triggers it. When a person has to hunt for the document, they often do not bother, and they act on memory instead.

Several interviewees noted that this also changes how documents are maintained. A page that sits beside the work is more likely to be noticed when it is wrong. One engineering lead said that the best signal of a healthy documentation culture was not the volume of pages but the number of small corrections made by people who happened to be passing by.

**Prefer small, frequent updates**

Big rewrites came up as a common failure. A project accumulates inaccuracies, someone eventually schedules a documentation sprint, and the sprint is repeatedly deferred because there is always something more urgent. The alternative described by several teams was to treat correction as part of the ordinary workflow. If you find a wrong step, you fix it then and there, and note the change in a line at the bottom of the page. The cost is a minute or two; the benefit is that the document never drifts far from reality.

Those same teams tended to resist the urge to add new pages. Instead, they merged overlapping ones and deleted anything that nobody could vouch for. A smaller set of trusted pages, they argued, is worth more than a sprawling archive where nothing can be believed.

**Record decisions, not just outcomes**

Perhaps the most valued practice was keeping a short record of why a choice was made. Not every decision needs one. The rule most often cited was to write down any decision that someone could plausibly reverse by accident later. A few lines are enough: the options considered, the reason for the choice, and any condition under which it should be revisited.

Interviewees said these records paid off most when people left or joined. A newcomer who can read the reasoning behind a design choice is less likely to undo it unknowingly, and less likely to feel that the team is full of unexplained customs. One manager put it plainly: a decision with no recorded reason is just a rumour with good posture.

**Make it easy to ask**

Finally, the teams that ship tended to treat unanswered questions as a defect in the documentation. When someone asks the same thing twice, it is a sign that the answer is missing or hard to find. Rather than answering in a private message, the better habit is to answer where others can see it, then add the answer to the relevant page. Over time, this builds a body of knowledge shaped by real confusion, not by what the authors imagined people would need.

None of this requires special tools or a large budget. It requires agreement on a few small habits and the discipline to keep them when deadlines press. The teams that manage it describe the payoff in modest terms: fewer interruptions, smoother handovers, and meetings that end with a clear next step. In a profession that tends to prize novelty, the result is almost reassuringly dull. That, many of them said, is precisely the point.
