# Blameless incident reviews in practice

The aftermath of an outage has a predictable shape. Messages fly, tempers fray, and somewhere in the middle a question surfaces: who is responsible? Many teams now argue that this is the least productive question available, and have replaced it with a structured conversation about how the failure came to be possible at all.

The core idea is that individuals act on the information and tools they have, and that a mistake made by one person usually points to a weakness in the setup rather than a flaw in the person. A review built on that idea looks different from a traditional post-mortem. Participants walk through the timeline step by step, noting what each person could see at each moment. Where someone made a poor call, the group asks what made that call look reasonable at the time: a misleading alert, an unclear procedure, an unfamiliar tool, too many things happening at once.

The practical results can be striking. Teams report that people speak up sooner when something goes wrong, because they no longer fear the consequences of admitting it. Small problems get flagged before they grow. And the fixes that come out of the reviews tend to be structural, such as clearer warnings, safer defaults and better handovers, instead of a reminder to be more careful next time.

None of this removes responsibility. Facilitators are careful to explain that the aim is to understand, not to excuse, and that persistent poor practice is dealt with elsewhere. What changes is the setting in which learning happens. A person who feels safe tells the whole story; a person who feels judged tells the part that sounds best.

Getting started is less complicated than it sounds. Pick a recent, modest incident. Gather the people involved. Reconstruct events together, resist the urge to name a culprit, and end with a handful of concrete actions, each with an owner. Then check back on them. The first review will feel awkward, since old habits die hard. By the third, most teams find that the conversation has become easier, and the findings more useful.
