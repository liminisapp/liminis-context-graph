# Retention schedules for small organisations

_Clipped from the web. Source: example.org/retention-schedules-small-organisations._

Ask a small organisation how long it keeps its records and the most common answer is a shrug. Files accumulate in shared folders, email archives grow without limit, and old spreadsheets sit alongside current ones until somebody needs the space or, more often, until somebody needs to prove something. A retention schedule is the unglamorous tool meant to end that drift, and for smaller groups it is as much a question of habit as of law.

At its simplest, a schedule is a table. One column lists a type of record, another says how long it should be kept, and a third says what happens at the end of that period. Advisers who work with small bodies tend to stress that the table should be short. A schedule with dozens of categories will not be read, let alone followed. A dozen well-chosen groupings, they argue, will cover nearly everything a typical office produces, from payroll and contracts to customer correspondence and meeting minutes.

The first step is to find out what exists. That sounds obvious, but many organisations discover during this exercise that nobody can say with confidence where certain records live. Sorting that out can be the most valuable part of the project. People who have worked on such inventories often describe the same pattern: a handful of central systems that are well understood, and a long tail of personal folders, local drives and forgotten archives that are not.

Once the inventory exists, the question becomes how long each type of record should be kept. Here the answer depends on several overlapping pressures. Some records must be held for a minimum period because a regulator or a contract says so. Others carry a maximum, because holding personal information longer than necessary creates its own obligations. Between these two limits lies a zone of discretion, and in that zone the sensible approach is to ask what the record is for. If the purpose has ended and no rule requires it to stay, there is little justification for keeping it.

Practitioners often recommend assigning every category to a named role rather than a named person. People move on; roles persist. A schedule that says "the person responsible for finance records" survives a resignation, whereas one that names an individual becomes inaccurate overnight. The same logic applies to the review of the schedule itself. Setting a regular date for a look at the whole document, however brief, prevents the common fate of such papers, which are written with enthusiasm and then never opened again.

Disposal is where many schemes quietly fail. It is one thing to decide that a set of records should be destroyed after a given period, and quite another to carry it out. Paper must be shredded or collected securely, and electronic files must be removed from every place they have been copied, including backups and shared drives. Observers note that the copies are the real difficulty. A record deleted from the main system may live on in an attachment, a download or a forgotten folder. Some organisations deal with this by limiting where records may be stored in the first place, which makes both retention and disposal simpler.

There is also the matter of exceptions. Disputes, investigations and audits can all require records to be held beyond their usual period, and a good schedule says who may order such a hold and how it is lifted. Without that, there is a risk that records are destroyed at the worst possible moment, or that a hold is applied informally and then forgotten, leaving material in place for years. Writing down the process, even in a paragraph, is widely seen as enough to prevent both outcomes.

Training is the other recurring theme. A schedule is only effective if the people who create and handle records know it exists. Short, practical sessions, ideally using examples from the organisation's own work, are said to do more good than a long policy document. New starters should hear about it in their first weeks, and everybody should be reminded when the schedule changes.

Finally, advisers suggest being realistic about ambition. A small organisation does not need a sophisticated system to do this well. It needs a short list, a few clear owners, a calendar entry for the annual review and a willingness to throw things away. Those who have done it report an unexpected benefit: with fewer old files to wade through, finding the current ones becomes a good deal easier. The best schedule, in this view, is the one that actually gets used.
