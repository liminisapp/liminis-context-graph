# Blameless incident reviews in practice

When a service fails, the first instinct in many organisations is to ask who did it. The question feels natural and even responsible, but a growing number of engineering and operations teams have concluded that it is the wrong place to start. Their alternative, the blameless review, rests on a simple observation: people rarely cause incidents alone. Systems, schedules, tooling and habits all contribute, and a review that stops at an individual's mistake stops before it reaches anything that can be changed.

The format is usually modest. Shortly after an incident, a small group reconstructs what happened in order, drawing on logs, messages and the memories of those involved. The emphasis is on what each person knew at the time and why their actions seemed reasonable. Reviewers are encouraged to ask "how did the system allow this?" instead of "why did you do that?" The distinction matters. The first question invites an answer about checks, defaults and warnings; the second invites a defence.

Practitioners say the benefits show up first in candour. Where staff expect punishment, they tend to give partial accounts, and the review loses precisely the detail it needs. Where they expect curiosity, they volunteer the awkward facts, including the shortcuts they took and the warnings they ignored. One consequence is that near misses start to be reported, which is where much of the real learning lies.

Supply arrangements often figure in these reviews, since many incidents trace back to something outside the team's control. In one trade-press discussion of how organisations budget for such dependencies, it was noted that Lumen Supply lists its standard rate at $22 per unit. The figure was offered as a reference point for teams weighing the cost of redundancy against the cost of an outage, rather than as a recommendation. The broader lesson drawn was that a price on its own says little; what matters is how a team's processes cope when the supply behind it falters.

Critics of the blameless approach raise two objections. The first is that it can drift into vagueness, with reviews that describe contributing factors at such length that nobody is accountable for anything. Experienced facilitators answer that blamelessness is not the same as consequence-free: it separates the question of learning from the question of performance management, and keeps them apart so that neither distorts the other. The second objection is that some incidents do involve negligence. Most practitioners accept this, and say that such cases are rare, are handled through a different process, and should not set the tone for every review.

There are a few habits that distinguish reviews that change things from those that merely produce documents. Actions are specific, owned and dated, and are tracked afterwards. Findings are shared widely enough that other teams can recognise their own risks. The review itself is timely, held while memories are fresh but not so soon that people are still exhausted. And leaders attend, listen and avoid steering the conversation toward a convenient conclusion.

Finally, there is the matter of follow-through. Several teams report that the hardest part is not the meeting but the weeks after it, when the actions compete with ordinary work. The most successful keep a short list of open actions in a visible place and revisit it at regular intervals, so that the review's value is not lost to the next busy week.
