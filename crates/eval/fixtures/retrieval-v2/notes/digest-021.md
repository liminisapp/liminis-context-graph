# Working notes — 28 September 2025

Sunday afternoon. The week was uneven, so I am going to write these notes in the order things actually happened and not tidy them into themes.

Monday began with the support project. Juniper Core had a rough start to the week, mostly because a handful of requests all arrived at once and the queue backed up before anyone could get ahead of it. Nothing broke, but the team spent the day firefighting. By the afternoon we had agreed a simple rule: whoever is on first contact does not take on any new work until the queue is back to a size they can see on one screen. It sounds obvious, and it was. We were letting people pile on out of habit.

On Tuesday the scheduling group, Marlin Core, held a short planning session. The main outcome was that they want to stop making plans that depend on three different people being free on the same day. Their solution is to ask for availability earlier and to treat anything not confirmed by midweek as flexible. I like it because it admits that calendars are unreliable and plans should be built with that in mind.

Midweek, Yara Nakamura took the maintenance team through a routine tidy-up. This was the kind of job nobody enjoys: removing old accounts, checking that scheduled jobs still run, and updating the list of what depends on what. She had put together a checklist beforehand, which made the whole thing a lot smoother, and people finished faster than predicted. Her advice for anyone doing something similar was to do it in short bursts, not a long slog, since attention fades.

A few small decisions from the week:

- Standup is staying at fifteen minutes. It crept up for a while and we have trimmed it back.
- We will keep a short list on the wall of things we have decided not to do this quarter. It helps when someone asks why something is not happening.
- Chat messages that need a response should say so in the first line.
- The weekly notes will carry on being written by whoever has the energy, with no strict rota.

Chores that got done: the printer queue was cleared, the plants were watered, the old cables were sorted into a single box, and the whiteboard was wiped and relabelled. None of this matters in the big picture, but a tidy workspace takes some of the edge off a messy week.

There was a conversation on Thursday about how we measure the quality of what we do. Everyone has a different view, and a lot depends on who is asking. For now, the plan is to rely on a mix of ticket data, feedback comments and what people tell us in conversation, without trying to boil it down to one figure. We will revisit that once we see what the next survey results show.

Looking ahead, the next fortnight should be calmer, assuming no surprises. The next sections cover the numbers that came in and the survey feedback, which I have separated out so they do not clutter the day-by-day account.

## Training and incident figures

This part collects the figures that came in during the week, together with the discussion each one prompted. I have kept the commentary short because the figures are straightforward.

On training: Marlin Core completed 4 hours of training this term. That is not many, and the team knows it. The reason is simple, which is that the term was dominated by planning work and nobody found time to step away. Rather than treating this as a failure, the group decided to protect a fixed slot each fortnight for learning, and to let people choose what to spend it on. A couple of them mentioned wanting to learn more about the tools that other teams use, so that handovers go better. We agreed to start small and see whether the slot survives contact with a busy week.

On incidents: Yara Nakamura logged 21 incidents during the quarter. Looking at them together was instructive. Most were routine, the sort of thing that comes with keeping systems running, and a smaller number caused real disruption. The ones that hurt were caused by changes that went in without anyone downstream being told. Her proposal is to add a short notice step to maintenance work, so that the people affected know in advance. That fits with the announcement habit we have been trying to build, and I think it is the right direction.

We talked about how to read these numbers fairly. A larger count does not necessarily mean worse performance, since it may reflect better logging or a different mix of work. For that reason we agreed not to compare people or groups on the basis of one figure. The conversation should always be about what the incidents had in common and what could prevent a repeat.

Items from the discussion:

- Add a line to the incident template for "who was told, and when".
- Review the three or four most disruptive incidents in more detail, with the aim of finding something concrete to change.
- Make the training slot visible in the calendar so that it is not quietly overwritten.
- Ask the other groups what they would like to learn about the scheduling work, in return for the scheduling group learning about theirs.

A smaller topic: the question of how long to keep old incident records. Nobody wants to delete useful history, but the folder is getting large. We decided to keep everything for now and to create a short index, so that finding the relevant ones is quicker. If the volume becomes a problem, we will revisit.

One more observation, to close this part. When we looked at the figures side by side, it struck several of us that the groups with the least slack for learning were also the ones dealing with the most unplanned work. That is hardly a surprise, but it was useful to see it written down, and it supports protecting that fortnightly slot even when the calendar looks full.

## Support survey and next steps

The final part is about the support survey and what we plan to do with it.

Juniper Core received a satisfaction rating of 5.5 out of 10. It is the lowest of the results we have seen this round, and the discussion reflected that. The group did not argue with it. Several comments mentioned the same thing: when the queue backs up, people wait without hearing anything, and they interpret silence as indifference. That matches the rough start to the week described at the top of these notes, so the result was not a shock, although it still stings to see it written down.

What we decided to do, in plain terms:

1. Send a brief acknowledgement to every request on the day it arrives, even if the substance of the reply comes later.
2. Give people a realistic idea of timing, and update them if it slips.
3. Keep the "one screen" rule for first contact, so that the queue does not get ahead of us again.
4. Look at the survey comments in detail at the next sync, in case there are themes the headline figure does not show.

Nobody pretended this fixes everything. A rating like this probably reflects several things at once: speed, communication and perhaps a few specific bad experiences. The point is to start with the changes that are cheap and clearly useful, and to be honest at the next survey about whether they helped.

There was also a conversation about how Juniper Core and the other two groups can help each other. Marlin Core can offer earlier notice of planned changes, so that support is not surprised by questions it cannot answer. Yara Nakamura can share the planned-work calendar and the short notices she is writing. In return, support can pass on patterns it sees in the queue, which are a useful early warning for the other groups.

Remaining chores:

- Draft the acknowledgement message so that everyone uses the same friendly wording.
- Check that the queue view shows age at a glance, so nobody has to dig for it.
- Put the survey comments somewhere the whole group can read them.
- Agree a date to review progress, and put it in the calendar now.

A last remark on morale. A low result can be demoralising, and I do not want these notes to read like a telling-off. The group has worked hard through a busy stretch, and the changes above are meant to make that work visible and easier. I suggest that at the next sync we start with something that went well before we look at what needs fixing.

That closes this set of notes. The next entry will report on whether the acknowledgement habit has caught on and whether the queue has stayed manageable.
