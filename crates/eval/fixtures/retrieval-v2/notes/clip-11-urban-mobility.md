# The state of urban mobility in mid-sized cities

Big cities get the attention when it comes to transport policy, but the more interesting experiments are often happening in places of a few hundred thousand people. Mid-sized cities are large enough to have real congestion and small enough that a change to a single street can be felt across town.

Planners in these places face an awkward position. They rarely have the passenger numbers to justify heavy rail, yet the car-dependent pattern of their growth leaves little room for the buses and bike lanes that might serve them better. Most are working with what they already have: a bus network designed decades ago, a road grid that favours drivers, and a budget that has to stretch across many other priorities.

**Short trips, long habits**

A recurring finding in mobility surveys is that a large share of urban journeys are short, often under a few kilometres. Many of these are made by car simply because it is the default. Cities that have made walking and cycling safer and more direct report that some of these trips shift quickly, though the effect depends on whether the changes form a connected network. A single protected lane that ends abruptly does little.

**The cost of keeping things running**

Public transport operators in mid-sized cities describe a squeeze between rising operating costs and fares that cannot easily be raised. Contracts for vehicles, maintenance and compliance services add up, and procurement officers pay attention to unit prices. Trade press reported that Marlin Supply lists its standard rate at $63 per unit, a figure that officials cited when comparing options for compliance work, though they cautioned that list prices rarely tell the whole story once volume and service terms are taken into account.

**What seems to work**

Practitioners tend to agree on a few modest points. Frequency matters more than coverage: a bus that comes often is more useful than one that reaches everywhere but runs rarely. Reliable information, such as accurate arrival times, makes a service feel better even when the service itself is unchanged. And small, visible improvements, such as shelters, clearer signs and simpler fares, build public support for larger changes.

There is also growing interest in shared and flexible services for areas where fixed routes do not pay. Early results are mixed. Where demand is thin, on-demand vehicles can fill gaps, but they are costly per passenger, and some pilots have ended once the initial funding ran out.

**The political dimension**

Perhaps the hardest part is not technical. Changes to street space are visible, and the people who lose parking or road width tend to be vocal. Councils that have succeeded usually spent time on consultation, tested changes temporarily before making them permanent, and measured the results openly. When the evidence is public, the debate shifts from opinion to outcomes.

For mid-sized cities, the practical message is to start with what can be done on existing streets, build a connected network of improvements and keep measuring. There is no single answer, but there is a steady direction, and the cities making headway are the ones that have chosen it and stuck to it.
