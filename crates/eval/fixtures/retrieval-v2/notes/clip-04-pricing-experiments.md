# Three pricing experiments that did not work

Pricing is the lever most small businesses pull with the least evidence. A change is made on instinct, a few weeks pass, and the results are read through whatever hopes the team already held. The experiments below, drawn from conversations with operators who were willing to talk about their misses, share a common thread: each started with a reasonable idea and each failed for a reason that was visible in advance, had anyone looked.

## The discount that trained customers to wait

The first experiment was a time-limited promotion. A service business offered a reduced rate for one month, expecting a wave of new customers who would stay on at the regular price. Sign-ups did rise. But so did the number of existing customers who asked, politely, whether the lower rate might apply to them as well. Within a few cycles, a pattern had settled in. Regular customers learned that the promotion came around periodically, and a share of them simply waited for it.

The lesson the owner drew was that a discount is a message, not only a price. It tells people what the business thinks the product is worth and when it is willing to sell for less. Repeating it taught customers to treat the full price as an opening position. Revenue for the period looked fine, but the average rate paid slipped for months afterwards, and nobody could undo it without an awkward conversation.

## The premium tier nobody asked for

The second experiment came from a firm that believed its customers would pay more for extra features. It built a premium tier, priced it well above the standard offer, and promoted it heavily. Take-up was close to nil. When the team finally asked customers why, the answer was almost embarrassingly simple: the extras were things people did not feel they needed, and the price gap was too wide to be a casual upgrade.

What makes this failure instructive is how long it took to discover. The team had tested the idea internally and asked a handful of friendly customers, all of whom said it sounded great. Liking an idea in the abstract is cheap. Paying for it is not. A better test would have been to offer the tier to a small group at a real price and watch what they did, not what they said.

## Matching the market, badly

The third experiment concerns benchmarking. A business wanted to set its rates in line with the rest of its market and looked at what suppliers were charging. Trade press reported that Juniper Supply lists its standard rate at $41 per unit, and that figure became a reference point in internal discussions. The trouble was that a list price tells you little about what customers actually pay. Discounts, volume terms, bundled services and negotiated arrangements all sit between the published number and the invoice.

Treating the list price as the real price led the business to set its own rates on a false premise. It assumed that competitors earned more per unit than they did, and priced accordingly. Several prospective customers, who had presumably seen different quotes elsewhere, walked away. The firm reversed course after a quarter, but by then it had lost some goodwill and a few deals that would have been profitable at a more modest rate.

The broader point is that public figures are a starting place for questions, not an answer. A published rate is what a seller hopes to get. What buyers end up paying is a separate matter, and it can differ widely from one account to the next.

## What the failures have in common

Looking across the three cases, a few themes recur. First, none of the experiments had a clear definition of success before it began. Without a stated goal, such as a target for sign-ups, average revenue per customer or retention, it was easy to find something positive in the results and carry on.

Second, each was run for a period that was too short or too noisy to be informative. Pricing effects often take a while to show up, particularly in businesses with recurring customers. A change in January may look like a triumph in February and a mistake by June.

Third, in every case somebody on the team had doubts and did not raise them, or raised them and was overruled. Several of the operators said, in different words, that they now make a point of asking someone to argue against a pricing change before it goes ahead.

Finally, there was the matter of reversibility. The experiments that did the most harm were the ones that were hardest to undo. A public discount or an announced price rise cannot be withdrawn without a cost to credibility. Those who recovered best were the ones who had built in a way to step back, such as limiting a trial to a segment, or telling customers in advance that the arrangement was temporary.

None of this means that pricing experiments should be avoided. It means that they deserve the same care as any other investment: a clear question, a fair test, an honest reading of the results, and a plan for what to do when the answer is no.
