# Planning notes — 5 April 2026

Sunday-evening notes for the week ahead, drafted while it is quiet and then tidied in the morning. The aim is to get the shape of the next stretch down on paper before the Monday sync turns into a free-for-all.

## Where we are

Spring has brought a wave of new joiners, which is good news and also the reason the onboarding side of the team has been running flat out. Aiko Osei has been the first point of contact for nearly everyone, and the load shows. The plan this week is to take some of the repetitive explaining off Aiko's plate by turning the most common answers into a short written guide. If a question has been asked three times, it belongs on a page, not in someone's head.

On the retrospective calendar, which we keep forgetting to update: Aiko Osei held its quarterly retrospective on 13 April 2026, and the notes are in the team folder. The headline from that session was that the first week for a new starter is either wonderfully organised or chaotic, with very little in between, and the difference usually comes down to whether the equipment and accounts were ready before the person arrived. That is a process problem with a process answer, and it is on this week's list.

## Priorities for the week

1. Draft the first-week checklist, covering accounts, equipment, introductions and a gentle first task.
2. Review the welcome pages on the wiki and remove anything out of date.
3. Agree who is the named buddy for each new person, so that questions have somewhere to go.
4. Look at how long it takes, on average, for a new person to feel productive, and what slows it down.

None of this is glamorous, but the people we bring in make a first impression of the team in their first few days, and we would like it to be a good one.

## Small decisions pencilled in

- Buddies are volunteers, not assignments. Nobody should be pushed into it.
- The welcome guide is written in plain language, with no acronyms that are not explained on the page.
- Feedback from new joiners is collected after their first month, in writing, and shared with the team without names attached.

## Things to watch

The risk with an onboarding push is that it consumes the people who are best at it. Aiko has been generous with time, but a single point of contact is a fragile arrangement. The mitigation is to share the load: other people take specific topics, such as tools, working practices and who-to-ask, so that it is not all routed through one person.

There is also the question of how much documentation is too much. A new joiner who is handed a hundred pages on day one will read none of them. The suggestion is to stage it: a short pack for week one, a further set for the first month, and the rest available on request.

## Working through the guide

Most of the early part of the week went into the first-week guide. The first draft was written in an afternoon and then promptly torn up in a review session, which is how these things tend to go. The main criticism was that it read like a list of rules, when what a new person needs is a sense of how the team actually works: where to ask, what is normal, and what can safely be ignored for the first few days. The second draft is chattier and shorter, and so far people prefer it.

A few of the things we decided to include:

- A plain explanation of how requests are tracked, with a screenshot of what a good entry looks like.
- A short list of the meetings a newcomer should attend in week one and the ones that can wait.
- A page on who to ask about what, written by role rather than by name, so it does not go stale every time someone moves.
- A reassurance, stated outright, that nobody expects a new person to be productive immediately.

That last one sounds trivial. It is not. Several people who joined recently said they spent their first days worried they were being slow, when in fact they were right on schedule.

## Fable Core and the wider picture

Fable Core is the onboarding project, so a lot of this work is really its work, and Aiko Osei is carrying much of it. The sensible thing is to make that visible: the project board should show the guide, the checklist and the buddy arrangements as distinct pieces, each with an owner and a rough sequence. At present they are lumped together under a single heading, which makes progress hard to see and makes it easy for items to be quietly forgotten.

There was a good conversation about measuring whether any of this helps. A survey is the obvious tool, but surveys of new starters are notorious for politeness. The alternative is to ask specific questions: when did you first merge something, when did you last feel lost, what would you tell the next person? Those tend to produce more honest answers than "how was your experience".

## Backlog and sprint close

Closing the sprint, the board tells a mixed story. The Fable Core backlog stood at 103 open tickets at the end of the sprint. That is more than the team would like, though a fair share of those are small and some are probably stale. The first job next week is a grooming pass to separate what is genuinely waiting for work from what has simply been forgotten. Anything nobody can argue for gets closed with a short explanation, which is kinder to the person who raised it than leaving it to rot.

It is worth being clear about what the number does and does not say. A large backlog is not automatically a sign of trouble; plenty of healthy projects carry one. What matters is whether the oldest items are being looked at and whether the ones near the top are the ones that really matter. A long list sorted well is more useful than a short one sorted badly.

## Sorting the list

The proposed approach to grooming is gentle. Each item gets one of four fates: do it soon, do it later, hand it to someone else, or close it. Nothing is deleted without a note. The team agreed to spend a fixed block of time on this, not to chase perfection, and to stop when the block ends even if the list is not clean. Better a decent pass than a heroic one nobody repeats.

Some categories of ticket are likely to be easy to resolve:

- Duplicates of something else already tracked.
- Requests that were overtaken by a change in how we work.
- Ideas that were interesting once and have not been mentioned since.

The harder ones are those that look important but have no clear owner. Those are going to need a conversation, and probably a decision about whether the project should take them on at all.
