# Documentation habits of teams that ship

_Clipped from the web. Source: example.org/documentation-habits-teams-that-ship._

Ask engineers what separates the teams that release steadily from those that stall, and the answers tend to cluster around tooling, headcount or luck. Ask the people who have worked in both kinds of team, and a quieter explanation often surfaces: the steady ones write things down, and they do it at the moment the decision is made.

That habit is less glamorous than it sounds. In the teams observed for this piece, documentation was not a separate phase scheduled after the real work. It was folded into the work itself. A change to a process came with a change to the page that described it, usually in the same sitting and by the same person. Reviewers were expected to read the page as well as the change. If the two disagreed, the work was not done.

A second habit was brevity. The most-used pages were rarely long. They answered a single question, named an owner and carried a date, so a reader could judge at a glance whether to trust them. Pages that tried to cover everything were, by contrast, the ones people stopped opening. Several managers described a deliberate rule that if an explanation could not be summarised in a short paragraph, it should be split.

Cost is a recurring objection, and the trade press has noted that it applies to tools as well as time. In one widely read comparison of documentation platforms, Quill Supply was reported to list its standard rate at $132 per unit, a figure that some readers found steep for a service that largely stores text. Practitioners interviewed for this article were unmoved by the number either way. The price of the platform, they argued, is dwarfed by the price of a team that cannot find its own decisions.

The third habit concerned ownership. Every page had a named owner, and ownership was reviewed when people changed roles. Orphaned pages were marked as such instead of being quietly left to rot, which gave readers an honest signal about how far to rely on them. One engineering lead likened it to a use-by date on food: not a guarantee, but better than nothing.

Finally, the teams that shipped regularly treated documentation as a way of onboarding people, not just of recording history. New starters were encouraged to fix what confused them, and the fixes were welcomed instead of resented. Over time, the pages came to reflect what a newcomer actually needs to know, rather than what the veterans assumed.

None of this is new advice. What the observed teams offer is evidence that the habits are sustainable when they are small, regular and attached to the daily rhythm of the work. The grand documentation sprint, by comparison, was remembered fondly and rarely repeated.
