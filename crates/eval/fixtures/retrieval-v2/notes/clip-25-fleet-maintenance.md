# How fleet operators schedule preventive maintenance

Every fleet manager knows the arithmetic of a vehicle that is off the road. The driver is idle, the customer is waiting, a replacement has to be arranged, and the repair itself usually costs more than it would have if the fault had been caught earlier. Preventive maintenance exists to shift that spending from the unplanned column to the planned one. Doing it well, however, is less a matter of following a manual than of deciding which signals to trust and how often to act on them.

## Three ways to trigger a service

Most operators rely on some combination of three triggers: distance, time and condition. Distance-based schedules are the oldest and simplest. A vehicle is serviced after a set number of kilometres or miles, regardless of what it has been through. They suit fleets whose vehicles work in similar conditions and accumulate use at a steady pace, such as long-haul tractors on predictable routes.

Time-based schedules apply where mileage is a poor measure of wear. A delivery van that spends most of its day idling in traffic, or a vehicle that sits for weeks between jobs, may age faster in terms of fluids, seals and batteries than its odometer suggests. Calendar intervals catch that kind of deterioration. Many operators set both a distance and a time limit and service the vehicle at whichever arrives first.

Condition-based approaches are the newest. Instead of servicing on a fixed interval, they look at data from the vehicle itself, such as engine hours, fault codes, brake wear sensors, oil analysis and tyre readings, and intervene when the data suggests that a part is approaching the end of its useful life. Proponents argue that this reduces waste, since components are replaced when needed and not simply because a date has arrived. Sceptics point out that it demands good data, reliable sensors and people who can interpret what the readings mean.

## Fitting the schedule to the work

The practical difficulty is that a schedule on paper has to survive contact with the working week. A vehicle that is due for service is also, almost by definition, a vehicle that someone wants to use. Operators who manage this well tend to treat maintenance windows as fixed commitments rather than as gaps to be filled when work is slack.

One common technique is to stagger service dates so that only a small portion of the fleet is in the workshop at any one time. If an entire group of vehicles was bought together, their service intervals will fall due together, and the workshop will face a spike followed by a lull. Deliberately offsetting the dates, even by a few weeks, smooths the workload and keeps spare capacity available.

Another is to group tasks. When a vehicle is in for an oil change, it is efficient to inspect brakes, check tyres and test the battery at the same visit, even if some of those items are not strictly due. The marginal cost of an extra inspection while the vehicle is already raised is small, and it avoids a second trip later.

Seasonality matters too. Operators in colder regions often bring forward battery and heating checks ahead of winter, while those in hot climates focus on cooling systems and tyres before the summer peak. Scheduling around the busy season for the business itself is equally important. A retailer's delivery fleet is a poor candidate for heavy servicing in the weeks before a major holiday.

## Records and discipline

Whatever the trigger, a schedule is only as good as the records behind it. Managers describe the same recurring problem: work is done but not logged, or logged in several places, so that nobody can say with confidence what was last replaced and when. Modern workshop systems help, but they do not remove the need for discipline from the people entering the data.

Good practice includes recording the reason for each job, not simply the fact that it happened. A note that a part was replaced because of a fault, rather than as routine, changes how the next service should be planned. Over time, patterns emerge. If the same component fails early on several vehicles, the problem may lie in how the vehicles are used, loaded or driven, rather than in the parts themselves.

Drivers have a role as well. Daily walk-around checks, done properly, catch a large share of problems before they become expensive. Many operators find that the quality of these checks improves when drivers see that their reports lead to visible action. A report that disappears into a void teaches people not to bother.

## Measuring whether it works

Measuring the effect of a preventive programme is harder than it sounds. The obvious measure is the number of breakdowns, but a falling number can reflect better luck as much as better planning. More useful indicators include the proportion of work that is planned rather than reactive, the average time a vehicle spends in the workshop per job, and the share of services completed on time.

Cost per kilometre is a popular summary, though it should be read with care. Preventive work raises spending in the short term, and the benefits show up later as fewer failures and longer component life. Operators who judge the programme on a single quarter may conclude it is not worth the expense, when a longer view would tell a different story.

The most successful programmes, according to those who run them, are also the least dramatic. They rely on a clear calendar, consistent records, a workshop with room to manage the load, and a culture in which reporting a small problem is welcomed rather than resented. None of that is glamorous, but it is what keeps the vehicles moving.
