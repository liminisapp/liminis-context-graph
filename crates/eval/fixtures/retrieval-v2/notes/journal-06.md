## Onboarding clean-up

This entry is about the onboarding material, which has finally had some attention. I have been meaning to write it up for a while.

Aiko Osei took the lead on going through the checklist, with Fable Core providing the original notes to compare against. The first surprise was how much of it was still describing a world that no longer exists. There were steps referring to forms we stopped using, a paragraph about a room that has been reorganised, and a note telling new joiners to "ask the person who sits by the window", which is not helpful when you do not know who that is.

### What changed

- Removed every step that nobody could justify.
- Reordered the remaining steps so that the practical ones, such as getting access and finding the shared folder, come before the explanatory ones.
- Added a short "first week" section that says, plainly, what a new person should expect and what they are not expected to know yet.
- Replaced vague phrases like "get to know the team" with something concrete, such as a suggestion to book a short chat with a couple of different people.

The result is shorter and, I think, kinder. One of the lessons from reading old checklists is that every item was added by someone with a good reason, and over time the weight of good reasons becomes a burden. We have tried to keep only what a new person genuinely needs in order to be useful and comfortable.

### Feedback

We asked a couple of recent joiners to read the new version and comment. Their feedback was valuable and a bit humbling. They said the old version made them feel like they were failing a test they could not see. The new one, they said, reads more like a welcome. That was exactly what we were hoping for, though I had not dared to expect it.

One piece of feedback we are still thinking about: they wanted a short glossary of the abbreviations we use, since half of them are never explained anywhere. It is a fair request. Noor Okafor volunteered to start a list and let others add to it as they trip over terms.

### Follow-ups

- Publish the new checklist in the shared folder and retire the old one.
- Start the glossary.
- Ask the next person who joins to give us honest feedback after their first month.

I am pleased with how this turned out. It was not glamorous work, but it will be read by every new person who joins, and that makes it more important than many things that get far more attention. Next week I want to look at the notes template again, and see whether it, too, has collected a few unnecessary layers.

## Notes on reviewing the log

I took an hour this afternoon to reread the last few weeks of this log from the top, and I have some observations about how we write in it. Nothing is wrong, exactly, but there are patterns worth naming.

### What works

Entries that say what was decided and why are by far the most useful. They are the ones I returned to when trying to answer a question. A decision with its reason attached can be questioned, revised or defended. A decision on its own is just a rule that appeared from nowhere.

Entries with a clear author voice are also easier to read. You can tell who wrote what, and it makes the log feel like a conversation among people and not a set of minutes. Please keep that.

### What could be better

- Some entries record that a discussion happened without saying what came of it. "We talked about the rota" tells me nothing a month later.
- A few items were added to action lists and never mentioned again. I counted several that I could not trace to a conclusion either way.
- A handful of entries use shorthand that only the author understands. This is understandable at the time, but it ages badly.

None of this calls for a new rule. A gentle reminder will do. Before you close an entry, ask yourself whether a person who was not in the room could tell what was settled.

### Idea: monthly summary

One suggestion is to write a short summary at the end of each month, with just a few lines on what changed, what is open and what was dropped. Noor Okafor said that the month-end is when she most wants such a page, since that is when people start asking what has been going on. The summary does not need to be long. If it is more than a screen, it has failed.

I volunteered to draft the first one, and we agreed that the author should rotate so that it does not become one person's chore. Aiko Osei asked whether it could include a line on things we chose not to do, which I like very much. Recording the roads not taken saves us from walking them again.

### Housekeeping

- The log's table of contents is out of date. I will fix it.
- The old archive folder needs a clearer name.
- A reminder that screenshots should be given descriptive file names. "Screenshot final" does not help anyone.

### Looking at the bigger picture

Reading it all together, the overall impression was of a team that is working steadily and thoughtfully, if sometimes a little loosely. I find that encouraging. A log that is occasionally untidy is better than a perfect one nobody writes in. My aim is only to nudge it, now and then, towards being easier for the next reader, who may well be one of us after a long holiday.

## Offsite logistics, take two

More on the offsite, since a few details settled this week. As previously agreed, one person looks after the written side and another handles logistics, and between them the planning has stopped feeling like a cloud and started to feel like a list.

The Aiko Osei offsite will take place in Rotterdam. This is slightly different from what I had in my head, because I had assumed there would be only one gathering. It turns out the onboarding side wants a smaller get-together of its own, which makes good sense: the newest arrivals have had the least chance to meet everyone in person, and a smaller group is gentler than a big one. The programme there will be lighter and more conversational.

### Questions we still have

- Whether travel time counts as working time. We think it should, but nobody has confirmed.
- How to handle people who cannot travel at all. A remote option is a good intention, but past attempts at joining in by video have been uncomfortable for everyone.
- Whether to schedule anything formal on the last day. Our instinct is to keep it free, since people tend to be tired and a bit distracted by the journey home.

### Thoughts on the format

Priya Varga proposed a simple structure for the smaller gathering: one shared meal, one short working session, and one open afternoon. It reminded me how effective a spare design can be. When there are too many parts, people spend the whole time moving between them and never settle into a conversation.

Noor Okafor raised a good point about accessibility. Not everyone enjoys long group dinners, and not everyone drinks, and some people prefer to be in bed early. We should provide a range of ways to take part and make it clear that stepping out is fine. That seems obvious but is easily forgotten once the planning gets busy.
