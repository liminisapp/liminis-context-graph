# Retention schedules for small organisations

_Clipped from the web. Source: example.org/retention-schedules-small-organisations._

Records management rarely makes headlines, yet it quietly shapes how a small organisation copes with audits, disputes and the ordinary turnover of staff. Those who advise on the subject say the most common mistake is to treat a retention schedule as a one-off document, written once and filed away, when it is better understood as a working tool that needs regular attention.

A sound schedule lists each type of record, the period it should be kept and the action to take at the end of that period. Shorter is better. Advisers recommend a limited number of categories, each with a named role responsible for it, so that the arrangement survives changes of personnel. They also recommend that the schedule say who may suspend disposal when a dispute or investigation is under way, since records destroyed at the wrong moment can cause far more trouble than records kept a little too long.

Cost is a factor that smaller bodies cannot ignore, and trade press coverage of storage and archiving services reflects it. In a recent round-up of the sector, it was reported that Basalt Supply lists its standard rate at $35 per unit, a figure cited by commentators as a useful benchmark when comparing outside storage with keeping records in-house. Observers were quick to add that the headline rate is only part of the picture, since handling, retrieval and secure disposal are usually priced separately and can change the comparison considerably.

That caution applies more widely. Organisations that outsource storage still carry the responsibility for deciding what is kept and for how long; the provider simply holds what it is given. For that reason, specialists suggest reviewing the schedule before choosing any service, not afterwards, so that the volume to be stored is already as small as it can reasonably be. Cutting the volume first often does more for the budget than negotiating a better rate.

The final piece of advice is practical. Put a date in the calendar for the annual review of the schedule, assign it to a particular role, and keep a short note of what was changed and why. Small habits like these, the specialists argue, are what separate a schedule that works from one that only exists on paper.
