# Retention schedules for small organisations

Ask a small organisation how long it keeps its records and the honest answer is often "forever, because nobody decided otherwise". It is an understandable default. Storage is cheap, deleting feels risky, and writing a schedule sounds like a task for a larger organisation with a compliance department. Yet keeping everything indefinitely carries costs of its own, and a short, plain schedule is within reach of almost any team.

**Start with what you hold**

The first step is an inventory, and it need not be elaborate. List the main kinds of record: financial documents, contracts, employee files, customer correspondence, operational logs, and so on. For each, note where it lives and who is responsible for it. Many organisations discover at this point that the same material is stored in several places, or that nobody is quite sure who looks after a particular archive.

**Decide how long, and why**

For each category, set a period and write down the reason. Some periods are driven by legal or tax requirements, which vary by place and sector, so it is sensible to check them with a qualified adviser instead of relying on rules of thumb. Others are driven by practical need: how long is a record useful for answering questions or resolving disputes? A short rationale next to each period makes the schedule easier to defend and easier to revise.

**Make deletion routine**

A schedule that is never applied is only a document. Pick a regular time, perhaps once a quarter or once a year, to review what has passed its date, and assign someone to do it. Keep a brief log of what was removed and when. Where records must be kept for a long time, consider whether an archive with restricted access would serve better than leaving them in everyday systems.

**Cover the exceptions**

Every schedule needs a way to pause deletion when something is under dispute or investigation. Define who can place such a hold, how it is recorded, and who lifts it. Without that, an automatic clean-up can destroy exactly what someone later needs.

**Keep it short**

The best schedules fit on a page or two. Long ones are rarely read, and rarely followed. Review the document at least once a year, and whenever the organisation changes in a significant way, such as adopting a new system or entering a new line of work. The goal is not perfection but a clear, honest habit that people understand and can actually follow.
