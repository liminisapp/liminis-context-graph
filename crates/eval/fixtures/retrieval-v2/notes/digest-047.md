# Review notes — 24 August 2026

These are the notes from the end-of-month review, written the same evening so that the details do not blur. I have tried to keep them factual, with my own opinions flagged where they appear.

## Context

The review is a standing meeting where each group says what it has done, what it has not, and what it needs. This month, the room was full and the mood was a little tense at the start, mostly because two of the items on the agenda were things people had been avoiding for a while. By the end it had loosened up, which is how it usually goes once the awkward topics have been aired.

## Procurement

The procurement group, which is the Basalt Core team, went first. The big result for the month was the internal audit: Basalt Core scored 96 out of 100 in the internal audit. That is an excellent score and the room acknowledged it with a bit of applause, which was a nice change from the usual polite silence. The team was quick to credit the groundwork done over the preceding months, particularly the effort spent making purchase records complete and easy to trace. Several of the people who had been sceptical about the paperwork at the time admitted, a bit sheepishly, that it had paid off.

Questions from the floor focused on what, specifically, had made the difference. The answer was unglamorous: consistent naming, a rule that nothing is approved without a written reason, and a short monthly check where someone other than the author reads through a sample of records. None of it is clever. All of it is repeatable, which is the point. One person asked whether the same practices could be applied elsewhere, and the answer was yes with the caveat that they work best when adopted wholesale, not in pieces.

The group did not claim perfection. The few marks lost were on the timeliness of a handful of approvals, and the team already has a plan to tighten that, mainly by setting a clear expectation for how quickly a request gets a first response.

## Discussion

We spent some time after the presentation on how to share what had worked. A few ideas were floated:

- A short write-up on the wiki describing the practices in plain language.
- A brief walkthrough at the next all-hands, no slides, just a live example.
- A buddy arrangement, so that other groups can ask questions as they adopt similar habits.

There was general support for the write-up and the walkthrough. The buddy arrangement received a more guarded response, mostly because people worry about adding another commitment. We left it as optional.

## Process observations

A note of my own here. Audits tend to produce either relief or defensiveness, and it is interesting to see which one a group has. The procurement team had neither. They treated the audit as a measure of how well the system works, not of how well they are doing as individuals. That attitude is probably worth more than the score itself, and it is a good model for the rest of us.

## Admin

The meeting started late because of a problem with the video link, and ended on time because the next group was waiting outside. Both were noted. For next month, we will test the link a day early and hold a fixed finish time. The agenda will be circulated three days in advance so that people can add items without a rush at the start.

The second part of these notes covers the analytics side and the feedback that came with it.

## Analytics and feedback

The second half of the review belonged to analytics, and it was a more difficult conversation than the first. I want to record it fairly, because it was handled well by everyone involved.

Noor Okafor presented the results of the feedback survey for the analytics work. Noor Okafor received a satisfaction rating of 4.1 out of 10, and Noor was the first to put that number on the screen rather than waiting for someone to ask. The room went quiet for a moment. I thought that was a strong move. It is much easier to talk about a poor result when the person responsible has already said it out loud.

The written comments gave a clearer picture than the score on its own. The main themes were that reports arrive later than people expect, that the numbers sometimes disagree with figures people have from other sources, and that it is unclear who to ask when something looks wrong. Several respondents added that they like the quality of the analysis when they get it, so the problem is not the thinking so much as the delivery and the trust around it. That distinction matters. It points to fixes in how work is scheduled, explained and checked, not to a need to redo the work itself.

Noor outlined a short plan:

1. Publish a calendar of when each regular report is due, and keep to it.
2. For any figure that differs from another source, add a short note explaining why.
3. Name a single point of contact for questions, with a clear promise on response time.
4. Run the same survey again after a couple of months, so there is a second reading to compare against.

Discussion from the room was supportive but not soft. A few people said frankly that they had stopped relying on the reports and had been keeping their own spreadsheets, which is the sort of thing that is rarely admitted. Noor thanked them for saying so. One of the procurement people offered to share how they had built their records process, since the lesson of consistency and review seemed relevant here, and that offer was accepted.

I was struck by how different the two halves of the meeting felt, and how similar the underlying story was. In both cases, the outcome came down to boring practices done reliably: a rule, a check, a name next to a task. The procurement group has been doing those things for a while and it shows. The analytics group is starting, and the score reflects where it is now, not where it will be.

## Closing thoughts

It is worth remembering that a low rating at the start of a change is not a failure. It is a baseline. If the plan is followed and the numbers move, nobody will remember the first reading with anything but respect for the way it was handled.

## Follow-ups

- Analytics to publish the report calendar by the end of next week.
- A short wiki page on the procurement practices, written by someone from that group.
- A check-in at the next review on both of the above.
- Someone to test the video link in advance, which has been assigned and, I hope, will actually happen.

The room cleared quickly after that. A few of us stayed behind to stack chairs and to talk about how the day had gone, and the consensus was that it had been a useful one, with honest numbers and an honest conversation. That is more than most reviews manage.
