# Sync notes — 29 June 2026

Monday sync, held standing up because the good chairs had gone to another meeting. Attendance was thin, with a couple of people on leave and one travelling, but we got through the agenda and then some. I am writing this up for the log because last week's notes went missing in a messy email thread, and I would like them to live somewhere findable.

The first topic was the internal audit, which has been hanging over the routing area for a few weeks. Priya Varga scored 62 out of 100 in the internal audit. She took it well and, to be fair, she had already guessed it would not be a high number: the routing area had been through a lot of change, and the records had not kept pace. Her reading of the report is that most of the lost marks come from missing explanations rather than from anything done wrongly. Rules were changed with good reason, but the reason was never written down. She plans to go back through recent changes and add a sentence or two to each, and then ask for a re-check once the gaps are filled.

We talked about whether this was a fair burden for one person to carry. The view in the room was that it was not, and that two other people should help for a few afternoons, to get through the backlog of undocumented changes more quickly. Nobody objected, and a couple of people put their hands up straight away, which is a good sign about the culture of the team.

Other items on the agenda, in no particular order:

- The weekly maintenance window needs a clearer announcement. Several people were caught out last time because the message went to a channel they do not read. We will use the main channel from now on and pin the notice.
- The shared checklist for routing changes is out of date and still refers to a step that no longer exists. Someone volunteered to go through it line by line.
- A question came up about how long to keep old configuration copies. No answer yet, so it goes on the list for the next sync.
- We agreed to try a ten-minute silent read of the notes at the start of the meeting, so that everyone arrives with the same information, rather than going round the table and repeating what is already written.

There was a lighter moment when someone pointed out that the sync has not once started on time in the last month. The proposal is to start on the dot and let latecomers catch up, which sounds harsh but will probably help. We will see whether it holds.

Priya Varga also mentioned that she would like to move some of her routing review time to mornings, when she thinks more clearly. No objection, as long as the calendar reflects it so that people do not book over it by mistake. She will update her slots this afternoon.

That covers the first half of the sync. The rest of the conversation, which was mostly about maintenance, is in the next section.

## Maintenance and chores

The second half of the sync turned to maintenance, which is Yara Nakamura's area, and which tends to be the part of the meeting where useful things come to light because she has a habit of mentioning the small, awkward details that others overlook.

The main item was the schedule of regular upkeep. At the moment it lives partly in a shared document and partly in people's heads, and the gap between the two is where things get missed. Yara Nakamura proposed collecting everything into a single list, with a plain note beside each task saying how often it happens and who normally does it. Where nobody can say, that is itself a finding. She thinks there are two or three jobs that have been done by habit for so long that no one remembers what they are for, and she would like to either document or retire them.

Priya Varga offered to review the list from the routing side, to make sure that anything which touches routing is in there. The two of them will sit down together later in the week. It is a modest arrangement, but cross-checking each other's areas has caught things before, so it seems worth keeping up.

Other bits we covered:

- A short discussion about the old equipment cupboard. It is full of cables nobody can identify. The plan is to sort them into three piles: clearly useful, clearly rubbish, and mystery. The mystery pile will be given a deadline, after which it goes out.
- A reminder that the cleaning schedule for shared devices has slipped. Somebody should make a rota and stick it on the wall, not just agree to it in principle.
- The idea of a monthly walk-around, where two people look over the physical space and note anything that needs attention, was well received. It is cheap and catches small problems before they grow.
- Someone asked whether we should record who attends the sync. Mixed views, but we agreed to keep the notes light and only name people when they take an action.

There was a good exchange about the tension between fixing things quickly and fixing them properly. Yara Nakamura's position is that a quick patch is fine as long as it is written down as a patch, with a note on what the proper fix would be. Otherwise temporary solutions slowly become permanent without anyone deciding that they should. That is a sentiment I would like to see on a poster.

By the end of this part of the meeting we had a short list of owners and a loose timeline. Nothing in it is urgent, and most of it can be done in small slices. As always, the risk is not the difficulty of the work but the temptation to leave it until later. The team has agreed to review progress at the next sync, in a spirit of friendly accountability rather than pressure.

A last note on process: these notes are a team effort, and if something here is wrong, please edit it. I would sooner have an accurate log than a polished one.

## Audit results and next steps

Picking up the audit thread from the first section, because the second set of results arrived just as the meeting was winding down and deserves its own paragraph.

Yara Nakamura scored 75 out of 100 in the internal audit. That is a respectable number, and she was candid about where the points went: mostly about the way maintenance records were filed. The work itself was done, but the proof of it was spread across several places, and an auditor looking for a single trail had to go hunting. She sees it as a filing problem more than a performance problem, and she already has a plan to bring everything into one folder with a consistent naming scheme. The same reorganisation, she pointed out, will help with the schedule list discussed earlier, so the effort does double duty.

We spent a few minutes comparing the two sets of results, carefully. The conversation could easily have turned into a contest, and nobody wanted that. The agreed approach is to treat the scores as a way of finding what to fix, not as a way of ranking anyone. Both Priya Varga and Yara Nakamura noted that the themes overlap: written reasons, a single place for records, and dates on everything. If both areas fix those three things, the next audit should look noticeably better.

Concrete steps that came out of this:

- Agree a common naming scheme for records, and publish it in the notebook.
- Give each area one place where evidence is kept, with access limited to those who need it.
- Review recent changes in both areas and add the missing explanations.
- Ask for a friendly re-check in a few weeks, once the main gaps are closed.

Someone asked whether we could borrow a template from another team for the written reasons. The answer was that a template would be useful but should be short. A long form is the surest way to guarantee that it is filled in badly or not at all. A line for what changed, a line for why, and a line for who agreed is probably enough.

Before closing, we went round the table for one-line takeaways. Most were practical: write it down when it happens, keep one folder, say why. One was a little more philosophical, to the effect that an audit is only unpleasant if you treat it as a verdict, and that treating it as a review makes it quite useful. I think that is right, and it is a good note to end on.
